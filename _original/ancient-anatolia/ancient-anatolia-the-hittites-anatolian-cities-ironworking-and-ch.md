<!-- block 13 role=content tool_mention -->
This lesson turns to Anatolia and the Aegean world just across the sea from it. This lesson meets the Hittites, whose empire grew to rival Egypt itself, clashed with it at the largest chariot battle in history, and then did something rarer than winning a war: signed the oldest surviving peace treaty in world history instead of fighting again.

<!-- section: ANATOLIAN BEGINNINGS -->

<!-- block 25 role=content -->
Anatolia had been farmed since long, long ago. Einkorn wheat, the world's very first tamed grain, was grown in this same region as far back as 9000 BC, part of the Fertile Crescent story of early farming. The Hittite state itself would not exist for thousands more years. Around 1650 BC, a Hittite kingdom began to pull power together across central Anatolia. It centered on the heavily fortified city of HattusaHAT-oo-sahthe massively fortified capital city of the Hittite Empire.. Hittite scribes borrowed **cuneiform** from Akkadian scribal custom, as cuneiform spread beyond Mesopotamia. They used it to write the Hittite language, the oldest known member of the Indo-European language family. That same broad family later includes Greek, Latin, and English.

<!-- section: AN EMPIRE BUILT BY CONQUEST -->

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
Hittite power reached its height under kings like Suppiluliuma I🔊soo-pih-loo-lee-OO-maha powerful Hittite king, ruling around 1350 BC, who expanded Hittite power deep into Syria., who around 1350 BC pushed Hittite rule deep into Syria. He beat the strong kingdom of Mitanni. This brought the Hittites into direct, sometimes hostile contact with Egypt's own sphere of power in the Levant. Hittite kings ruled as both political and religious heads of their state. They dealt with Egypt's pharaoh as self-declared equals. Both used the title "Great King." Only a small handful of the era's most powerful rulers could fairly claim that title for themselves. Historians sometimes call this small circle of rulers the Amarna system, named for a cache of clay letters found in Egypt. Those letters record the "Great Kings" of Egypt, Hatti, Babylon, Assyria, and Mitanni. They wrote to each other almost as equals, even while they were rivals and sometimes fought.

<!-- section: KADESH: THE LARGEST CHARIOT BATTLE IN HISTORY -->

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
That rivalry between Hatti and Egypt came to a head in 1274 BC. The two powers clashed at the **Battle of Kadesh**, fought in modern Syria. Egypt's army was led by Ramesses II🔊RAM-uh-seezpharaoh of Egypt from about 1279 to 1213 BC, who led Egypt's army at Kadesh and later signed peace with the Hittites., only in the fifth year of a reign that would eventually last sixty-six years. The Hittite army was led by Muwatalli II🔊moo-wah-TAH-leethe Hittite king who commanded Hittite forces against Ramesses II at Kadesh.. Both sides sent out thousands of horse-drawn chariotsCHAIR-ee-uhta light, two-wheeled cart pulled by horses, used in war for fast attacks and quick movement., with modern estimates for the two armies combined reaching five to six thousand chariots in all. This likely makes Kadesh the largest chariot battle ever fought in world history. Both empires' own records claim victory, a useful reminder that royal writings rarely give a fair, two-sided account of their own wars. What really followed the battle was neither a clear Egyptian win nor a clear Hittite one. Years of tension followed. At last came something neither side had won through years of fighting.

<!-- section: BATTLE KADESH -->

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
Fought in 1274 BC, the Battle of Kadesh pitted Ramesses II's Egyptian army against Muwatalli II's Hittite forces, each fielding thousands of chariots. Neither side won a clear victory on the field, but the battle's real legacy came fifteen years later, in the peace treaty it eventually produced.

<!-- section: A DIFFERENT KIND OF ENDING -->

<!-- block 42 role=content -->
In 1259 BC, Ramesses II and the new Hittite king Hattusili III🔊hah-too-SEE-leethe Hittite king, and Muwatalli II's younger brother, who later signed the peace treaty with Ramesses II. signed a formal peace deal. It was the **Egyptian-Hittite Peace Treaty**, the oldest surviving peace treatyTREE-teea formal written agreement between two states, often ending a war or setting the terms of peace. in world history. Rather than one side simply beating the other, both empires chose to talk out a deal instead. Years of costly fighting neither side could clearly win had worn them both down. The treaty set out clear rules. Neither side would attack the other. Both sides promised to help defend each other against outside threats. Each side agreed to send back people who had fled to it. Years later, Hattusili III's own daughter married Ramesses II, sealing the peace between the two empires with a royal marriage as well as a written deal. This is worth holding up as a real case study in how lasting peace really gets made. Two worn-out great powers, having fought as much as either could stand, chose the harder, less exciting work of talking out peace over the costs of more war. Both judged that peace served them better than any further fighting could.

<!-- section: CITIES HATTUSA -->

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
HattusaHAT-oo-sahthe massively fortified capital city of the Hittite Empire. was ringed by massive stone walls more than four miles long, pierced by gates decorated with carved lions and a sphinx. Thousands of cuneiform tablets found in its ruins recorded the Hittite Empire's laws, treaties, and religious rituals.

<!-- section: IRON, RECONSIDERED -->

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
One more Hittite feat deserves a closer look: early ironworkingthe skill of heating and shaping iron into tools and weapons, a harder and eventually more common metal than bronze.. For decades, popular stories described the Hittites as guarding a strict monopolymuh-NOP-uh-leetotal control over making or selling something, with no real competition. on iron-making skill. In these stories, iron was a closely held state secret, kept hidden from rival kingdoms. More recent study has changed this picture a good deal. Iron-working knowledge during the Hittite period seems to have grown more slowly than that old story claimed. It also seems to have spread more widely across the region than the "secret monopoly" story allowed. One striking example, a dagger with an iron blade and a gold handle, was found in a royal tomb at Alaca Höyük in central Anatolia, dating to around 2500 BC, centuries before the Hittite state itself existed. This shows iron was already known and prized as a rare, precious metal long before anyone learned to make it in any real amount. Iron did not fully replace bronze until later, once the shocks of the Bronze Age Collapse cut off the long-distance tin trade that bronze production depended on. The Hittites deserve credit as early, skilled ironworkers, but not for a tightly guarded monopoly the evidence no longer supports.

<!-- section: TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT IRONWORKING -->

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
Iron has one big advantage over bronze: it does not need tin, a metal that had to be hauled in from distant mines and made bronze production dependent on long, fragile trade routes. Early Hittite smiths worked with iron by at least 1400 BC, though it stayed rare and costly, more a prestige metal for gifts and ceremonial objects than an everyday replacement for bronze weapons. Turning raw iron ore into usable metal took much higher furnace heat than bronze-making required, along with repeated hammering to drive out impurities. Only after the Bronze Age Collapse cut off the tin trade did iron slowly become the practical, everyday metal of choice across the ancient world.
