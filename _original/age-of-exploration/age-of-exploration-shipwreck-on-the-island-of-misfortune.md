<!-- section: No gold, and no way home -->

<!-- block 35 role=content -->
The leader was a Spanish commander named Narváez. In 1528, he landed in Florida with about three hundred men. He had been told that gold waited inland. He marched into the country and found swamps, hunger, and fights with the people who lived there. There was no gold and no great city.

<!-- block 36 role=content -->
**Cabeza de Vaca** served as treasurer, which meant that he kept track of money and goods for the king. He was not the leader, and he was not the man who made the big choices. Yet the choices he lived through shaped the rest of his life. A man who started out counting the king's gold ended up with no gold and almost nothing to wear.

<!-- block 37 role=content -->
The Spaniards met strong resistance from the Apalachee people of northern Florida. Many men died from arrows and disease. The expedition had come to rule a land, and it could barely feed itself.

<!-- block 38 role=content -->
After months, the men were sick and starving. They decided to build boats and sail along the Gulf coast to reach Spanish towns in Mexico. They had no shipbuilders and few tools, so they made what they needed from what they had. They melted their iron stirrups and spurs to make saws and axes. They sewed their shirts into sails. They made rope from palm fiber and horsehair, and they used horse hides to hold fresh water. They ate their horses as they worked.

<!-- block 39 role=content -->
In the fall of 1528, five crowded boats set out. Storms, thirst, and hunger pulled them apart. Narváez himself was lost at sea. In November, two boats washed ashore on a sandy island off what is now the Texas coast. Among the survivors was Cabeza de Vaca, the expedition's treasurer. He named the island **the Island of Misfortune** (*Malhado*).

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
Imagine being on one of those boats. Thirst was a constant danger, and there was little fresh water to drink. The boats sat low in the water, and waves washed over the sides. Nobody on board had ever sailed this coast, and nobody could say how far it was to the nearest Spanish town.

<!-- section: People who moved with the food -->

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
The island was not empty. People lived along this coast, and they are often called the **Karankawa**. The Spaniards did not use that name, and the groups they met had names of their own. These coastal people did not farm. They lived by the water, and they moved with the food.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
Here is how it worked. In the cooler months, families lived on islands and shores where they could catch fish and gather shellfish and roots. In warmer months, many moved inland to gather berries and the fruit of the prickly pear cactus, and to hunt. Their lives followed a calendar of food. This pattern is part of the **ecology** of the Gulf coast. Their way of life was **seasonal**, and it worked well for people who knew the coast.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
These families traveled by canoe, carved from single tree trunks. They crossed the shallow bays and channels between the islands and the mainland, and they knew every inlet and shell bank. The Spaniards, with their heavy gear and clumsy boats, knew almost nothing about the coast. That difference mattered a great deal.

<!-- block 52 role=content -->
Knowing the coast was a form of wealth. A family that knew where the shellfish were thick in winter and where the berries ripened in summer would eat well. A family that did not would go hungry. The Spaniards had arrived in the worst possible state, with no skill in the one thing that mattered most on that shore.

<!-- section: First fed, then enslaved -->

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
Winter came hard. The Spaniards were weak, cold, and starving. They had no ships and no weapons that mattered. The people of the island gave them food and fire. Without that help, the Spaniards would have died within weeks.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
Then things turned. A sickness spread among the island people, and some of them blamed the strangers. Most of the Spaniards died that winter. The few who lived were scattered among different groups. Cabeza de Vaca himself was **enslaved** for years. The men who had come to rule the land now lived at the mercy of the people they had meant to rule.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
It helps to remember how little the Spaniards could offer in return. They had no goods to trade and no skills the island people needed. A group that is already short of food has to think hard before it shares with strangers. The surprise is not that the kindness ended. The surprise is that it began.

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
Of the men who landed on the island, only a handful lived through the winter. Cold, hunger, and sickness killed the rest. The coastal people were neither helpless hosts nor fierce enemies in this story. They were people making choices about food, fear, and family, just as the Spaniards were.

<!-- block 59 role=content voice -->
The words of Cabeza de Vaca help us here, because he wrote down what he lived through. He told of Spaniards so hungry that some did terrible things to stay alive. He also told of Native families who wept when the strangers suffered. His book shows both sides, and that is what makes it so useful to historians.

<!-- section: A model with no system to take over -->

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
Think back to Mexico. There, Spain found a city of 200,000 people and a long list of towns that owed payment to the ruler. Here, there was nothing like that. There were no fields of corn to tax, no towns to govern, and no ruler to replace. There was no **tribute** to collect. The Spaniards could not command anyone. They needed the local people to survive, because those people knew the land.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
This is a hint about the whole story of Spain in Texas. The Spanish model needed a large, settled population. On the Texas coast, Spain found people who moved with the seasons and who owed nothing to anyone.

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
The shipwreck also shows how much luck shaped these events. A storm decided where the boats came ashore. A sickness decided how the island people treated the strangers. A few small turns, and the story would have ended in silence.

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
It is also a reminder that this story survives only because a few men lived. If they had not been fed, nobody would ever have heard of the island or of what happened there. Cabeza de Vaca wrote it all down, and his story continues as he walks across Texas.
