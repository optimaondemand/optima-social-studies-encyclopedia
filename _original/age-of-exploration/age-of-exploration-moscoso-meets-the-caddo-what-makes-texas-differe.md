<!-- section: A country of farms -->

<!-- block 38 role=content -->
Another Spanish army, led by **De Soto**, had spent three years marching through the southeastern United States. In 1542, De Soto died near the Mississippi River. An officer named **Moscoso** took command. The men now wanted only to go home. Moscoso led them west, hoping to find a land **route** to Mexico. A Portuguese soldier known as the Gentleman of **Elvas** wrote an account of the march.

<!-- block 39 role=content -->
By now, the army had lost many men to battles and sickness. The survivors had marched for years, and they were thin and tired. They needed food, and they needed a way out.

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
In East Texas, they met the Caddo. The Spaniards walked into a country of farms. They found villages surrounded by fields of corn, beans, and squash. People lived in tall houses with grass roofs. After a long time with little food, the army found plenty.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
To see why this mattered, think about what the Spaniards had been eating. Their supplies had run out long before, and many of the men had been hungry for months. A village with stored corn was a rescue. At the same time, the Caddo had to decide how to treat an army of strangers. The Caddo had good reason to be careful.

<!-- section: Settled, farming, and organized -->

<!-- block 50 role=content voice -->
The Caddo lived in one place and farmed the land. Their villages were permanent. They also joined their towns together under shared leaders, which we call a **confederacy**. There were three large Caddo confederacies. This was a society with farmers, leaders, trade routes, and ceremonies. It was very different from the coastal peoples who moved with the seasons, and from the **nomads** who followed the **bison** across the plains.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
Each Caddo community had a leader called a **caddi**. The Caddo also built large earthen mounds for ceremonies and for the homes of important people. Their potters made beautiful bowls and jars, and their traders carried goods over long distances.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
A mound was not just a pile of dirt. It took many people working together to build one, and that took planning, leaders, and shared beliefs. A society that could do this was well organized. It could feed many people, settle disputes, and hold ceremonies that brought distant towns together. Spaniards who expected to find simple villages were wrong.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
Each of these ways of life made sense. The coastal families used the sea and the shore. The plains hunters used the herds. The Caddo used rich soil and steady rain. None of these peoples was backward or simple. Each had worked out a way to live well in the place where it stood, and each way took generations of skill.

<!-- section: Four regions and a rainfall line -->

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
Texas is not one kind of place. Geographers divide it into four large regions. The Gulf Coastal Plains run along the coast and up into the east. The North Central Plains lie in the middle. The Great Plains cover the high, flat grasslands of the Panhandle and the west. The Mountains and Basins lie in the far west.

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
What ties the regions together is rain. In the far east, more than 50 inches of rain fall in a year. In the far west, fewer than 10 inches fall. Between the two, the rainfall drops steadily from east to west. This is a rainfall **gradient**. Rain decides which plants will grow, and what grows helps decide how people can live. That is a large part of why the Caddo farmed in the east, and why the bison herds roamed the drier plains.

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
Farmers need steady rain, or a river that can water their fields. West of the middle of the state, farming without that water is very hard. That helps explain why the villages of farmers lie in the east, and why the people of the dry plains followed animals instead of crops.

<!-- section: Check the map -->

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
Some maps and movies show Texas as one huge dry desert dotted with tall cactus. The big cactus with arms, called the saguaro, does not grow in Texas at all. Find a picture or map like that and ask what it leaves out. Which of the four regions does it show, and which does it hide? What would a student from East Texas say about it?

<!-- section: No second Mexico -->

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
What did Spain want? It wanted a second Mexico. That meant a large settled population to put to work, a **tribute** system to take over, and wealth to carry home. The Caddo had a settled population, but their villages were far from Mexico, and there was no silver or gold. In the rest of Texas, the Spaniards found no cities and no tribute at all.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
As the Spaniards went west from the Caddo towns, they found that the land grew drier. The Gentleman of Elvas wrote that the people there lived in small groups, hunted for their food, and had no corn. The army had little to eat and no good guides. Moscoso decided to turn back.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
This was the verdict, and it came from the land as much as from the people. Spain's model needed a tribute system. East Texas had farmers but no gold. West of there, the land was too dry to feed a large population. A model built for one kind of place was failing in another.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
After Moscoso turned back, his men built boats on the Mississippi and sailed to Mexico. Coronado had already gone home. Spain had its answer. There was no second Mexico in Texas. For about the next 140 years, Spain mostly turned away from this land. It would turn back in 1685, but only because someone else arrived first.
