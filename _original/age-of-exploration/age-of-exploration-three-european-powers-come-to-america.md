<!-- section: A long way to the money -->

<!-- block 38 role=content -->
In the 1400s, Europeans wanted goods from Asia. Silk, gold, and **spices** like pepper and cinnamon all came from far away. Merchants carried them across land and sea until they reached Europe. The trip was long, and every trader along the way took a share of the profit. By the time the goods arrived, they cost a lot.

<!-- block 39 role=content -->
Spices were not just for flavor. Some were used as medicine, and all of them were light, easy to carry, and very valuable for their weight. A ship that carried a full load of pepper could make its owners rich. That is why so many people wanted a cheaper way to bring spices home.

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
Then the **Ottoman Empire** grew strong. Its armies took the great city of Constantinople in 1453, and the Ottomans came to control many of the land **routes** to Asia. Europeans could still trade, but they paid more, and they had to deal with a powerful **rival**. Kings and merchants began to ask a simple question. Was there a way around?

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
The Ottomans did not seal the door. They taxed the trade and made it harder to plan. A merchant who could not trust what goods would cost next year began to look for other options. Pressure like this does not force anyone to do one particular thing. But it makes people willing to try ideas they would have laughed at before.

<!-- section: Portugal shows the way -->

<!-- block 43 role=content -->
Portugal, a small country on the Atlantic coast, tried the sea. Its sailors built better ships and learned to find their way with the stars and new tools. This skill is called **navigation**. Step by step, Portuguese ships sailed farther down the coast of Africa. In 1498, a Portuguese captain named **Vasco da Gama** reached India by sailing all the way around Africa. Portugal had found a sea route to Asia, and it was not going to share it.

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
Every ship that came back from India loaded with spices made Portugal richer, and the rulers of Spain were watching closely. If Spain wanted a share, it would need a route of its own.

<!-- section: Spain tries a different road -->

<!-- block 49 role=content -->
Spain wanted a route of its own. An Italian sailor named Christopher Columbus had an idea. He would sail west across the Atlantic and reach Asia from the other side. Many experts thought the ocean was far too wide, and they were right. Columbus believed the distance was much shorter than it really is. Even so, Queen Isabella and King Ferdinand of Spain agreed to pay for his **expedition**. One of the men who helped find the money was a royal official named **Santángel**.

<!-- block 52 role=content voice -->
You may have heard that Columbus was trying to prove the Earth is round. That is a myth. Educated Europeans already knew the Earth was round. The argument was about how big it was. Columbus guessed that Asia was close. The experts guessed that it was far away, and the experts were right.

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
In 1492, Columbus set out with three ships. After more than two months at sea, he reached an island in the Caribbean. He believed he had arrived at the edge of Asia, the lands Europeans called the Indies. So he called the people he met "Indians." The name stuck, even though he was wrong about where he was.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Columbus was also lucky. If the Americas had not been in his path, his ships would probably have run out of food and water in the middle of the ocean. One of the most important voyages in history could have ended with a few lost ships and a sad story told in Spanish harbors. Nothing about the outcome was certain.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
Think about what Columbus needed to make his plan work. He needed money, ships, and a ruler willing to gamble. Isabella and Ferdinand had just finished a long war to drive the last Muslim kingdom out of Spain, and their **treasury** was thin. Still, the possible reward was huge. A rich new route to Asia could pay for a great deal.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
After Columbus returned in 1493, he wrote a letter to Santángel describing what he had found. The letter was copied and printed, and the news spread across Europe. Soon every ruler wanted to know more.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
Columbus made four voyages in all. He died in 1506 still believing that he had reached the edge of Asia. He never understood that he had found two continents that Europeans did not know about.

<!-- section: The land was already full of people -->

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
Columbus had not found an empty world. Millions of people lived in the Americas, and they spoke hundreds of languages. Some lived in small bands that hunted and gathered their food. Others were farmers with villages and large fields of corn. Some ruled cities as big as the largest in Europe, as the story of Mexico will show.

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
Historians cannot agree on exactly how many people lived in the Americas in 1492. The estimates are very different, but nearly everyone agrees on one point. It was many millions.

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
This was a problem no European king had planned for. Spain, France, and England all wanted to claim land. But who owned it? And what should a European power do about the people who already lived there? Should it trade with them, fight them, put them to work, or push them away?

<!-- section: Each power starts in a different place -->

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
Spain moved first, and it moved south. Its ships and soldiers settled the islands of the Caribbean and then pushed onto the mainland. That story is told in other lessons on Spain in the Americas. England sent an Italian sailor named John Cabot across the North Atlantic in 1497. He sailed for the English king and reached the coast of North America far to the north. France sent ships along the same coast. In 1534, **Cartier** sailed into the great St. Lawrence River, and France began to build a hold in the north.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
Each of these three empires would try its own answer to the problem of the people already here. And each answer would turn out to fit some places better than others. That is a story worth following. Eventually, all three answers would meet in a land called Texas.
