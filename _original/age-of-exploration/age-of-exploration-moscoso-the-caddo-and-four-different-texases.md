<!-- section: GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY -->

<!-- block 3 role=content -->
**Querecho** (keh-REH-cho) — the name Coronado's expedition used for Apache bands living on the southern plains before they had horses.

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
**Caddo** (KAD-oh) — a Native American people of East Texas who farmed the land and lived in permanent, organized settlements.

<!-- block 5 role=content -->
**confederacy** (kun-FED-er-uh-see) — several separate communities joined together under a shared leadership or agreement.

<!-- section: If Texas were really one big desert, none of this would make sense. -->

<!-- block 8 role=content -->
While Coronado searched the plains, a second Spanish expedition, the surviving column of Hernando de Soto's men under Luis de Moscoso, reached East Texas in 1542. What they found there, the Caddo, could not have been more different from the Querecho Coronado had just met. Both discoveries point to the same lesson: Texas was never one place.

<!-- section: PART 1 A DIFFERENT SOCIETY ENTIRELY -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
The **Caddo** of East Texas farmed the land, lived in permanent settlements, and organized themselves into a **confederacy** of related communities. This is about as far from the mobile, plains-based life of the Querecho as two Texas societies could be, and both existed at the same time, in the same colony-to-be.

<!-- block 18 role=content -->
Pairing the Querecho and the Caddo in the same week is not an accident. The variation between them is itself the evidence for the thesis: there was never one Texas for Spain to figure out. There were several, and each required a different approach entirely.

<!-- section: PART 2 CRITIQUE THE MAP -->

<!-- block 20 role=content tool_mention -->
Some real published curricula group Texas together with Oklahoma, New Mexico, and Arizona as one region, "the Southwest," and illustrate it with a saguaro cactus. Use the interactive below to see what that picture leaves out, and why it is not just simplified but wrong.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW -->

<!-- block 22 role=content -->
There are no saguaros in Texas. That cactus grows only in the Sonoran Desert of Arizona and northern Mexico, a different desert than the one in far West Texas. And most of Texas is not desert at all: Houston averages about 50 inches of rain a year. Seattle averages about 38. A "Southwest" label that reaches for a saguaro to represent Texas is not simplifying the state. It is describing a different one.

<!-- section: The verdict -->

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
Between Coronado's grass houses and Moscoso's confirmation that East Texas held nothing like Tenochtitlán either, Spain reaches a verdict: no second Mexico exists in Texas. This is not a failure to keep searching. It is an honest acceptance of what the search actually found, and Spain turns its attention elsewhere for a long time to come.

<!-- section: THE CENTRAL QUESTION -->

<!-- block 28 role=content -->
No second Mexico exists, and Spain turns away for over a century. But the variety, four regions, wildly different societies, does not disappear just because Spain stops looking. Keep it in view: it is about to matter again.
