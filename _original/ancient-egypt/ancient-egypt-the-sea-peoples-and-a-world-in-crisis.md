<!-- section: WHO WERE THE SEA PEOPLES? -->

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
Egyptian records from this period describe repeated attacks by a shifting group of seaborne raiders and migrants. Modern historians call them the Sea Peoplesa shifting coalition of seaborne raiders and migrants, named by modern scholars, blamed for attacks on Egypt and other powers during the Bronze Age Collapse.. This is a label invented by nineteenth-century scholars, not a name these groups used for themselves. Egyptian writings name several distinct peoples within this coalition. Egyptian scribes called them the Peleset, Sherden, Shekelesh, Denyen, and Weshesh. The Peleset most of all are widely thought to be the ancestors of the Philistines. Their exact origins are still debated among historians today. They may have come from the Aegean, Anatolia, the central Mediterranean, or several places at once. What is not in doubt is the timing. These attacks grow sharply worse in these very same years. This is exactly when the Hittite Empire, Mycenaean Greece, and Kassite Babylon all collapse or suffer heavy damage.

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
Around 1208 BC, shortly before this wider crisis, the pharaoh Merneptah🔊MER-nep-tahpharaoh of Egypt from about 1213 to 1203 BC, whose victory stele contains the earliest known mention of "Israel." had already fought off a coalition of enemies. It included Libyan raiders and groups later linked to the Sea Peoples. He marked his win on a stele. That stele happens to hold the earliest known mention anywhere of a people called "Israel," living in CanaanKAY-nunthe region along the eastern Mediterranean coast, roughly modern Israel, the Palestinian territories, and Lebanon.. But the more serious crisis was still to come. The Medinet Habu inscription of Ramesses III🔊RAM-uh-seezpharaoh of Egypt from about 1186 to 1155 BC, who repelled a major Sea Peoples assault., describes a much larger Sea Peoples attack reaching Egypt's own borders roughly a generation later, one Egypt survived, but at lasting cost.

<!-- section: A CASE STUDY IN HISTORIOGRAPHY -->

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
This is a moment where being honest about the evidence matters more than telling a tidy story. It would be tempting to tell this story as one clean tale: one cause, one villain, one clear turning point. The evidence will not support that kind of story. Historians have proposed drought and long-lasting famine, weakening royal treasuries and pushing groups to migrate and raid. Others point to political failure at home, revolts, and breakdowns in the complex trade networks that bronze production itself depended on. Bronze needs tin, and tin had to travel huge distances to reach Bronze Age workshops. Fragile palace economies depended on steady tribute and trade flowing in all the time. They may have been quick to fall apart once any one link in that chain snapped. Still others describe a kind of systems collapsea breakdown in which several linked problems (drought, war, broken trade routes) combine and make each other worse, rather than one single cause bringing down a whole civilization., in which several of these pressures combine and feed each other. In this picture, no single cause alone would have been enough to bring down so much at once. Several causes together, though, proved too much to stop. The honest, current answer from scholars is that several of these factors were likely working together. Just how they combined is still not fully understood, and it may never be settled with full confidence.

<!-- block 33 role=content tool_mention -->
This is one of the clearest examples anywhere in the ancient world of how messy real historical change can be. There is no march toward sure progress here, no plan unfolding as it should. Great powers, the Hittite Empire above all, simply stop existing within a matter of decades. Others, Egypt and Mesopotamia among them, survive, deeply wounded. They never become quite the same civilizations they were before. At the trading city of Ugarit in Syria, archaeologists found a letter from the king's own officials still baking in a kiln, a plea for ships and help that was never sent in time before the city burned. History does not owe anyone a tidy explanation. A good historian resists making one up where the evidence will not support it.

<!-- section: EGYPT, WOUNDED BUT ALIVE -->

<!-- block 35 role=content -->
Ramesses III's forces did succeed in beating back the Sea Peoples assault, in a major land and sea battle the Medinet Habu inscription describes and celebrates in vivid detail. Egyptian carvings show ships locked in close combat, archers on both riverbanks, and captured Sea Peoples warriors bound and paraded before the pharaoh. One likely Egyptian tactic was to draw enemy ships into the narrow, shallow channels of the Nile Delta itself. There, the enemy ships had little room to turn or flee. Rows of Egyptian archers on the banks could then fire down on them at close range.

<!-- block 36 role=content -->
But victory came at a real cost. Egypt's New Kingdom survived the crisis, but its money, land, and standing abroad never fully came back. The following century sees Egyptian power steadily fading.

Egyptian carvings show Sea Peoples warriors wearing tall feathered or spiked headdresses, kilts, and round shields, arriving with entire families and ox-carts loaded with belongings, a picture of migration as much as invasion.
