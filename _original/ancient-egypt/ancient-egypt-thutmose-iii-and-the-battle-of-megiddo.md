When Hatshepsut died, her stepson Thutmose III became sole ruler and chose a very different path to greatness: the battlefield. His first campaign, against a rebel coalition at Megiddo, produced the earliest battle in world history with a detailed surviving account, and it turned on one risky decision his own officers argued against.

<!-- section: A REBELLION IN CANAAN -->

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
A coalitionkoh-uh-LISH-una group of separate rulers or states that join forces for a shared goal, such as a rebellion or a war. of Canaanite and Syrian rulers had gathered at the fortified city of **Megiddo**, in modern northern Israel. They were backed by the strong kingdom of Mitanni to the northeast. Together, they rose up in open revolt against Egyptian rule. Egyptian records claim the coalition included the chiefs of over 330 towns and cities across Canaan and Syria, a sign of just how big this revolt was. Thutmose III marched his army north to face them. His route took about ten days, moving up the coast road Egyptians called the Ways of Horus. The official Annals of Thutmose III, recorded on temple walls at Karnak, tell what happened next in rare detail for an ancient war record.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW CONTINGENCY AT THE MOUNTAIN PASS -->

<!-- block 29 role=content -->
This is worth pausing on as a real historical contingency, in the spirit of Sargon's basket and Hammurabi's late-life wars. Thutmose III's own officers thought the narrow pass was reckless. By ordinary war logic, they had a point. Suppose the coalition army had simply posted a small guard at the pass's far end. The exposed, single-file Egyptian column could have been badly hurt before ever reaching open ground. Nothing about this outcome was set in stone ahead of time. A different set of scouts, a slightly different coalition plan, or a pharaoh less willing to overrule his own generals, and the history of Egypt might record a very different first campaign for Thutmose III.

<!-- section: HISTORICAL FIGURE THUTMOSE III -->

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
Thutmose IIIthoot-MOH-suhpharaoh of Egypt from about 1479 to 1425 BC, Hatshepsut's stepson and successor. ruled Egypt from about 1479 to 1425 BC, spending his early years as co-regent under Hatshepsut before becoming sole pharaoh at her death. He led as many as seventeen military campaigns into the Levant, earning a lasting reputation as one of Egypt's greatest warrior-kings.

<!-- section: A LASTING GRIP ON THE LEVANT -->

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
Megiddo was only the beginning. Over the next two decades, Thutmose III returned to the Levant again and again. Local rulers who had once rebelled were now forced to send tribute to Egypt each year. Some were even made to hand over their own sons to be raised at the Egyptian royal court. Those sons, once grown, sometimes went home as rulers loyal to Egypt. This was a quiet but strong way of binding a conquered region to the empire without constant war. By the end of his reign, Egypt controlled more land than at any earlier point in its history, stretching from Nubia in the south to the Euphrates River in the north. Thutmose III kept careful written lists of the tribute he collected each year, recording exact counts of gold rings, cattle, timber, and captives from every subject city, a habit of precise record-keeping that echoes the Mesopotamian scribal traditions of Sumer, Akkad, and Babylon.

<!-- section: BATTLE MEGIDDO -->

<!-- block 36 role=content -->
Fought around 1457 BC, the Battle of Megiddo is the earliest battle in world history with a detailed surviving account. Thutmose III's risky choice to march his army through a narrow mountain pass, rather than a safer road, caught the rebel coalition by surprise and helped decide the outcome before the fighting even began.

<!-- section: TWO PHARAOHS, TWO VIRTUES -->

<!-- block 38 role=content voice imperative -->
Set beside Hatshepsut's Punt trip, Thutmose III's Megiddo campaign offers a clear contrast in what ancient greatness could look like. Hatshepsut sought careful rule, patience, and strong peace-making. Thutmose III sought a bold gamble he led in person, growing Egyptian power by force. Neither path was clearly the right one. Both rulers are remembered, in their own ways, as strong pharaohs.
