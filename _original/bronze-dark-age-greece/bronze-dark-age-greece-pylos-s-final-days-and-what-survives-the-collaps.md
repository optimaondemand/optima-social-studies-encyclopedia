<!-- section: What does ordinary office paperwork look like in a civilization's final weeks? -->

<!-- block 9 role=content tool_mention -->
Ramesses III's Medinet Habu inscription tells the Bronze Age Collapse's story from the winning side. This lesson tells the same crisis from the losing side, through the ordinary paperwork of a Mycenaean palace government at Pylos that had no idea it was living through its final weeks, and takes stock of what the collapse swept away and what survived it.

<!-- section: THE VIEW FROM PYLOS -->

<!-- block 21 role=content -->
At **Pylos**, one of the Mycenaean palace centers, archaeologists recovered a set of clay tablets. The American archaeologist Carl Blegen began digging at Pylos in 1939. He found the very first tablet on his very first day at the site, a stroke of luck that quickly led to hundreds more. These tablets were hardened and preserved, almost as a strange gift, by the very fire that destroyed the palace around 1180 BC. Nearly a thousand tablets survive from Pylos alone, one of the largest caches of Linear B writing ever found. Among them are records historians call the **rower tablets**: lists organizing men, likely rowers for warships, and assigning officers to watch posts along the coastline.

<!-- block 22 role=content voice -->
These are not poetry. They are not royal boasts. They do not even clearly state what danger the palace expected. They are exactly the kind of dry, practical bookkeeping a **palace economy** produced all the time as a matter of routine: names, numbers, place assignments. Read alongside what we now know happened next, they carry an unmistakable weight. This was a government mobilizing its coastal defenses. It was most likely acting in real fear of an attack from the sea, in the final months before its palace burned and Mycenaean rule in this region ended for good. One tablet, known to scholars simply as An 657, lists rowers assigned watch duty at points all along the coast, group by group, the closest thing this archive offers to a battle plan.

<!-- section: HISTORICAL FIGURE CARL BLEGEN -->

<!-- block 25 role=content -->
**Carl Blegen** (BLEG-en) was an American archaeologist who began digging at Pylos in 1939 and found the site's first Linear B tablet on his opening day of work, leading to one of the largest collections of Linear B writing ever recovered.

<!-- section: A GOVERNMENT THAT DID NOT KNOW ITS OWN ENDING -->

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
This is worth sitting with directly. Whoever wrote these tablets was doing ordinary office work. It was the same kind of work Linear B scribes had been doing for a long time: counting men and assigning posts. They had no sense that they were recording the last acts of a civilization. Other tablets from these same final months record ordinary religious offerings, oil and wine sent out to local shrines, exactly as they had been every year before. History rarely announces its own turning points in advance. The people living through the Bronze Age Collapse felt it, in the moment, as a string of ordinary decisions, urgent threats, and office responses. They did not call it "the Bronze Age Collapse," a label historians would only apply looking backward, centuries later, once the full pattern of loss across a whole region became clear. No scribe at Pylos ever wrote that word for what was happening to them, because from where they stood, nothing yet had a name.

<!-- section: CITIES PYLOS -->

<!-- block 30 role=content -->
**Pylos**, on the southwest coast of the Greek mainland, was a Mycenaean palace center whose ruler tradition later called Nestor. Its clay archives, baked hard by the very fire that destroyed the palace, preserve one of the largest collections of Linear B writing ever found.

<!-- section: WHAT ENDURES, AND WHAT DOES NOT -->

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
It is worth taking stock of what this crisis really swept away, and what it did not. The Hittite Empire ceases to exist as a political power. **Hattusa** itself is destroyed and never rebuilt as an imperial capital. Mycenaean palace civilization collapses so completely that writing itself vanishes from Greece for centuries. Its wanax-centered chain of officials and its Linear B writing disappear together. Other Mycenaean centers, including Tiryns and Mycenae itself, burn in this same rough span of years, though not always in exactly the same year. This is a reminder that even a single collapse comes in pieces, not all at once. Greece would not write in any form again for roughly four hundred years, until a new alphabet, borrowed from Phoenicia, took hold around 800 BC. This is the period later called the Greek Dark Age. Some estimates suggest Greece lost as much as three-quarters of its people over the century that followed.

<!-- block 33 role=content -->
Kassite Babylon falls as well. This opens up a Mesopotamian power vacuum, as new powers rise to fill it. Egypt and the rest of Mesopotamia survive, wounded, their reach and wealth smaller than before, but their civilizations continue. **Cyprus**, an island rich in copper just off the Levantine coast, comes through this period more intact than many of its neighbors. Its valuable copper trade was likely too useful for the wider crisis to fully cut off. The Levant, meanwhile, is resettled in these same troubled decades by new arrivals. These include the Philistines, likely tied to the Sea Peoples themselves. They also include the tribal groups that would become the kingdom of Israel. And along the coast, one people is placed, almost by accident of survival and where it stood, to inherit much of the long-distance Mediterranean trade network Mycenaean sailors once ran. That people is **Phoenicia**.

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
Nothing about any of this was planned, or owed to any civilization by the shape of history itself. Some things ended. Some things endured, changed by what they survived. Some new things were only now becoming possible. Within a century, the Near East would look nothing like the world before the collapse.
