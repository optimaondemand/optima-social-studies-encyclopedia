<!-- section: How does a war begin over a small cannon? -->

<!-- block 38 role=content tool_mention -->
In the fall of 1835, a Mexican officer rode into the Texas town of **Gonzales** to take back a small **cannon**. The townspeople said no. Within two months, **Texian** **volunteers** had defeated a Mexican army in San Antonio. This lesson follows how a quarrel over one cannon became a war, and what the colonists said they were fighting for.

<!-- section: Come and take it -->

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
In 1831, the Mexican government had given the town of Gonzales a small cannon to defend itself against **raids**. Four years later, relations with Mexico were tense. The commander of the soldiers in Texas wanted the cannon back. He feared that the colonists might use it against Mexican troops, and he sent a lieutenant named **Castañeda** with about a hundred soldiers to collect it.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
The people of Gonzales said no. They buried the cannon in a field, then dug it up and mounted it on wheels. They painted a white banner with a picture of the cannon and four words, "Come and Take It." When the Mexican soldiers arrived at the river, the Texians were waiting on the far bank.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
Volunteers from nearby towns hurried to join them. By October 2, about 150 Texians were gathered. At dawn, they fired the cannon and charged. There was a short **skirmish**. A single Mexican soldier was killed, and the Mexican troops pulled back. Very few shots were fired, but the shooting had started.

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
Think about how small the spark was. Nobody in Gonzales wanted a war over a cannon. But the cannon had become a symbol. To the colonists, giving it up meant giving up the right to defend themselves. To the Mexican officer, letting them keep it meant letting a **colony** set the rules. Neither side could easily back down.

<!-- section: An army of the people -->

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
News of Gonzales spread quickly. Volunteers streamed to the town, and they chose Stephen F. Austin to lead them. Austin had just returned from prison in Mexico City, and he did not want war. But he accepted the job, because he believed that his neighbors needed a leader they trusted.

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
The Texians marched on the Mexican **garrison** at **Goliad** and took the fort at **La Bahía** without much fight. Then they turned toward San Antonio, where General **Cos** had gathered Mexican troops. The Texian army was an odd force. It had farmers, merchants, lawyers, and boys, and its supplies were poor. But the men knew how to shoot.

<!-- section: A fight from the riverbank -->

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
On the night of October 27, about ninety Texians under Jim Bowie and James Fannin camped near the old Concepción **mission**, south of San Antonio. They chose a spot beside the river, where a deep, steep cutbank gave them cover. Soldiers behind a cutbank could load and fire without being easy targets.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
At dawn on October 28, General Cos sent hundreds of soldiers against them, with a cannon. Fog covered the field at first. When it lifted, the Mexican soldiers advanced across open ground in plain view. The Texians fired from behind the riverbank, one careful shot after another. They drove back charge after charge.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
By the end of the fight, the Mexican army had lost dozens of men, and the Texians had lost one. The result astonished everyone. Ninety men with **rifles** had beaten a force many times larger. Word of the victory raised the hopes of Texians across the colonies.

<!-- block 71 role=content voice imperative -->
Notice why the Texians won. The Mexican army was brave and well trained, but it fought in the open. The Texians had good rifles and good cover. A soldier behind cover can aim carefully, and a soldier in the open must keep moving and cannot fire back easily. This idea about cover will matter again in later battles.

<!-- section: Ben Milam leads the way -->

<!-- block 74 role=content -->
The Texian army settled outside San Antonio for a **siege** through November. The weather turned cold, supplies ran low, and many volunteers went home. Austin left to serve as a representative to the United States, and Edward Burleson took command. In early December, the officers voted to give up and **retreat**.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
Then Ben Milam stepped forward. He was an old frontiersman and a veteran of earlier fights in Mexico. On the morning of December 5, he called for volunteers to follow him into the town. About three hundred men agreed. They attacked before dawn and began to fight house by house through the streets.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
For four days, the Texians dug through walls, climbed onto roofs, and drove Mexican soldiers back toward the old **Alamo** and the main plaza. Milam was killed by a sniper on December 7. Others took his place. On December 9, General Cos asked for terms. The Mexican soldiers **surrendered** and agreed to leave Texas.

<!-- block 79 role=content voice imperative -->
Look at the choices that led to this moment. Austin could have refused to lead. Milam could have gone home with the rest. The Texians could have waited for the Mexican army to starve them out. Each choice went the other way, and by the end of the year the colonists held the main town of the **province**.

<!-- block 80 role=content -->
By the middle of December, there were no Mexican soldiers left in Texas. Texians held San Antonio and the Alamo with it. It seemed that the war was over, and that the colonists had won. They had no way of knowing that the largest army was still on its way.

<!-- section: What were they fighting for? -->

<!-- block 82 role=content -->
In November, while the army besieged San Antonio, delegates met at **San Felipe** in a meeting called the **Consultation**. They had to decide what the war was about. Many wanted **independence**. But most did not. The Consultation declared that Texians were fighting to defend the Mexican Constitution of 1824, which **Santa Anna** had thrown out.

<!-- block 86 role=content -->
That choice had a purpose. By fighting for the old constitution, Texians could claim to be loyal Mexicans who defended the law. They could also hope that **liberal** Mexicans in other states would join them. A fight for the constitution could draw **allies**, but a fight for independence would draw only enemies.

<!-- block 87 role=content -->
The Consultation set up a **provisional** government, with Henry Smith as governor and Sam Houston as commander of the regular army. It was a temporary government meant to last until a better one could be built. But the leaders quarreled with each other almost at once, and the volunteers answered to different chiefs.

<!-- block 88 role=content -->
The plan for a campaign shows the confusion. Some leaders wanted to attack the city of **Matamoros**, at the mouth of the **Rio Grande**, and join Mexican **federalists**. That plan made sense only if the war was about restoring the constitution. Nobody would plan such a trip if the war had already become a war of separation.

<!-- block 92 role=content -->
That is the question that the next months would answer. In December 1835, Texians still said that they were fighting for Mexico's constitution. By March, they would be fighting for something very different, and the men at the Alamo may not have known which war they were in.
