<!-- section: The Old Three Hundred -->

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
Austin's first colony was meant for three hundred families. The settlers who signed up early became known as the Old Three Hundred. Most of them received the papers for their land in 1824. They did not all arrive at once. Families came by boat along the coast and by wagon from Louisiana, and they built their first homes in the winter of 1821 and 1822.

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
Nearly all of them came from the American South. They were from Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana, a **region** that people at the time called the Old Southwest. These were farm families who knew how to clear land, plant cotton and corn, and live on a **frontier**. To them, Texas looked like more of the same land, only cheaper.

<!-- block 42 role=content -->
Think about how big a step this was. These families left friends, churches, and family graves in the United States. They crossed a border into a country with a different language and a different government. They did so because they believed the gain was worth the risk, and that belief was strong enough to carry them through hard years.

<!-- block 43 role=content -->
Money was a main reason. After the panic of 1819, many families in the United States were in **debt** and had little hope of buying good land. In Texas, they could receive a large tract for a small fee. A farmer could dream of owning more land than his father ever had.

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
Some settlers brought **enslaved** people with them. By the middle of the 1820s, roughly one in four people in the colony was enslaved. Slavery was part of the world these settlers came from, and cotton farming made it more valuable. Mexican law was uneasy about slavery, but the colony kept it in place, and that choice would cause trouble later.

<!-- section: What Mexico asked -->

<!-- block 46 role=content -->
Mexico set clear terms. A family that farmed could receive about 177 acres. A family that wanted to raise cattle could receive a **league** of land, which is about 4,428 acres. A family that did both could receive both tracts. The land cost almost nothing. A small fee paid for the surveying and the paperwork.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
In return, settlers had to promise to obey Mexican law. Each settler took an **oath** of loyalty to Mexico, and the law said that settlers had to be Catholic. In practice, there were almost no priests in the colony, and few settlers were asked to prove much about their faith. The requirement was more a matter of form than of daily life.

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
Mexico did not pay Austin a salary for his work as **empresario**. His reward came from land and from fees paid by the settlers. This gave him a strong reason to keep the colony growing. It also gave the settlers a reason to watch how he used his power.

<!-- section: Between the rivers -->

<!-- block 54 role=content voice imperative -->
Look at where the colony sat. It stretched between the **Brazos** and the **Colorado**, from the Gulf coast northwest into the rolling prairie. Rivers were the highways of the time. Boats could carry cotton to the coast, and settlers could build farms along the banks where the soil was rich. Austin's capital, **San Felipe**, sat on the Brazos.

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
The northern edge of the colony followed an old Spanish road, the King's Highway. This was not an accident. The road crossed the rivers at the places where they could be **forded**, which means that they were shallow enough for wagons to cross. A **boundary** that lay where people already traveled was easy to find and easy to defend.

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
Rainfall mattered even more than the rivers. Near the town of Bryan, at the northern edge of the colony, about 41 inches of rain fall in a year. That is enough for cotton and corn without special help. The coast gets even more. Settlers who stayed between the rivers found the weather and the soil that they knew from home.

<!-- section: West of the Colorado -->

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
Ranching country is a different world. Cattle need open grass and water more than they need rain at planting time. Spanish ranchers had worked this country for a century. Later settlers would learn from them, and a new kind of Texas would grow up west of the cotton farms.

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
That is why Austin's colony took the shape it did. It sat where the rain and the rivers favored the kind of farming the settlers already knew. Farther west, they would have had to learn a different way of life. Settlers tend to stop where the land looks familiar, and the first colony stopped where it felt like home.

<!-- section: Hard work, slow growth -->

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
Settling was hard. The first families faced hunger, mosquitoes, floods, and sickness. Some left. Those who stayed cleared land with axes and oxen, planted corn first so that they would have food, and waited for the cotton to come in. A colony of cabins and cornfields takes years to turn into a community.

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
Austin kept a close eye on every family. He wrote to settlers, advised them where to build, and settled quarrels over land. He wanted a colony that would last, and he knew that a few unhappy families could damage its name in the capital. His careful work is part of why the colony grew steadily while other schemes failed.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
In its crops, its labor, and its customs, the colony was the western edge of the American South, set down inside Mexico. The Mexican government had hoped for loyal farmers who would hold a dangerous frontier. It had received a piece of the Old Southwest instead.
