<!-- section: Why hold a fort you know might fall? -->

<!-- block 4 role=content tool_mention -->
Jim Bowie was sent to evaluate whether the Alamo could actually be defended or should be abandoned. His letter of February 2 answers the question directly: he and the garrison "will rather die in these ditches" than give up the post. Understanding why this specific fort was worth that choice is the whole point of today's lesson.

<!-- section: PART 1 A PICKET GUARD, NOT A LAST STAND FOR ITS OWN SAKE -->

<!-- block 13 role=content -->
Béxar was judged worth holding for two practical reasons: it functioned as a picket guard, a forward post shielding the settlements further east from a surprise advance, and it was a fort worth bargaining with if it came to negotiation. This was not a symbolic stand chosen for its own sake; it was a defensible calculation about where a small force could do the most good.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW -->

<!-- block 15 role=content -->
Juan Seguín's Tejano company served inside the Alamo garrison alongside the Anglo defenders. This conflict crossed ethnic lines from the start; it was not simply Anglos against Mexicans as two uniform sides.

<!-- section: PART 2 THIRTEEN DAYS -->

<!-- block 17 role=content tool_mention -->
The siege ran from February 23 to March 6, 1836. Travis wrote his famous letter on February 24, the second day, asking for help "with all dispatch." Try the interactive below to see how the numbers on both sides changed as the siege went on.

<!-- block 20 role=content -->
On March 2, four days before the fort fell, independence was declared at Washington-on-the-Brazos, roughly 150 miles away. George Childress drafted that Declaration. The garrison at the Alamo almost certainly never learned of it before the final assault. They may well have died still fighting, in their own understanding, for the Constitution of 1824.
