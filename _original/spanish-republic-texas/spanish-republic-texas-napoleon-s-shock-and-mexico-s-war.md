<!-- section: Whom do the colonies obey now? -->

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
In 1808, Napoleon deposed the Spanish Bourbon monarchy and installed his own brother on the throne. That single event threw Spain's American colonies into a real crisis of legitimacy: the king they owed allegiance to had simply been replaced. Mexico's war for independence begins inside that crisis, and Texas becomes one of its battlegrounds.

<!-- section: PART 1 THE GRITO AND THE WAR REACHES TEXAS -->

<!-- block 13 role=content -->
In 1810, the priest Miguel Hidalgo issued his call to revolt, remembered as the Grito. Fighting soon reached Texas directly: the Gutiérrez–Magee expedition of 1812 and the Republican Army of the North carried the war onto Texas soil. In 1813, the Battle of Medina became the bloodiest battle ever fought in Texas, and the province was left wrecked in its aftermath.

<!-- section: PART 2 WHAT MEXICO ACTUALLY INHERITED -->

<!-- block 15 role=content -->
Mexico won its independence in 1821. What it inherited in Texas was not a thriving, populous province. It was three small towns and effectively no working control over the vast territory between them, the direct legacy of the slow Bourbon reforms and the sudden shock of Napoleon's invasion arriving together.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
The slow reforms had already hollowed out Spain's frontier capacity long before 1808. The sudden shock is what finally triggers the chain of events ending in 1821, but it did not create the underlying weakness by itself.

<!-- section: THE CENTRAL QUESTION, ALMOST ANSWERED -->

<!-- block 19 role=content -->
Three hundred years of the Spanish model produced three towns and a wrecked frontier. The hinge comes next: a new government looks at exactly this inheritance and makes a decision that changes everything.
