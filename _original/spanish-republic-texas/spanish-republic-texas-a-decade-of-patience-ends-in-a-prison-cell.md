<!-- section: A decade of patience ends in a prison cell. -->

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
Austin carried the 1833 convention's petitions south to Mexico City and won a real, if partial, victory: the repeal of the Law of April 6. Then a single letter, written in frustration, undid all of it.

<!-- section: GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY -->

<!-- block 14 role=content -->
**conciliation** (kun-sih-lee-AY-shun) — the practice of settling a disagreement peacefully, through negotiation and compromise, rather than through force.

<!-- section: PART 1 A REAL WIN, RIGHT BEFORE THE FALL -->

<!-- block 16 role=content -->
Austin carried the Convention of 1833's petitions south to Mexico City and met with Santa Anna directly. He achieved a genuine, if **conciliation** -minded, partial win: repeal of the hated Law of April 6, 1830. Right up until the letter that triggered his arrest, his patient, decade-long diplomatic approach was still producing real results.

<!-- section: PART 2 ONE LETTER, INTERCEPTED -->

<!-- block 18 role=content -->
On October 2, 1833, frustrated by the pace of the process, Austin wrote to San Antonio's town council urging it to begin organizing a separate state government without waiting for Mexico City's approval. The letter was intercepted before it accomplished anything. Austin was arrested at Saltillo in January 1834 and imprisoned in Mexico City.

<!-- section: WATCH OUT -->

<!-- block 20 role=content -->
Do not read this letter as Austin suddenly abandoning conciliation. It was written out of frustration in a single moment, after years of the opposite approach. The irony is real: the colony's most patient negotiator gets arrested for the one moment he was not.

<!-- block 21 role=content -->
Austin was released on bond that December and finally made it home to Texas in September 1835, nearly two years after he had left. The man who had spent a decade defending conciliation as the right approach came home convinced it had finally failed.
