<!-- section: What happens when a French ship misses its target by hundreds of miles? -->

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
In 1685, René Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, sailed to found a French colony at the mouth of the Mississippi River. He landed instead at Matagorda Bay, on the Texas coast, hundreds of miles off course. That single error is about to set Spain and France on a collision course that changes Texas permanently.

<!-- section: PART 1 THE FRENCH MODEL IN PRACTICE -->

<!-- block 13 role=content -->
Recall the French model: send few settlers, trade, ally, intermarry. In practice, along the St. Lawrence River in Canada, this meant a fur trade built on relationships with Native nations, not conquest or mass settlement. La Salle's 1685 expedition was meant to extend this same pattern south, founding a post at the mouth of the Mississippi to secure French control of the river's trade.

<!-- section: PART 2 FORT ST. LOUIS COLLAPSES -->

<!-- block 15 role=content -->
Instead of the Mississippi, La Salle's ships reached Matagorda Bay on the Texas coast, hundreds of miles to the west. Rather than correct course immediately, the expedition built a settlement there anyway: Fort St. Louis. It did not last. Disease and grueling labor killed colonists at a devastating rate, supplies ran short, and discipline broke down entirely. In 1687, La Salle was murdered by his own men during an overland expedition to seek help.

<!-- section: WATCH OUT -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
La Salle's murder is not simply an adventure story. It is a case of a colony's own self-government failing under pressure, worth taking seriously as a caution rather than a footnote.

<!-- block 18 role=content -->
What remained of Fort St. Louis limped along until 1689, when the Karankawa, whose territory this was, finished what disease and mutiny had already nearly completed.
