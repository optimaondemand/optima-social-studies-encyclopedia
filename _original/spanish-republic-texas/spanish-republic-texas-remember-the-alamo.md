<!-- section: A fort at San Antonio -->

<!-- block 46 role=content -->
After the victory in December, most of the Texian army went home. A small garrison stayed in San Antonio, in the old mission chapel and walled yard that people called the Alamo. The place had thick walls, but they enclosed a very large space. It would take many men to defend it, and there were never enough.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
Think about the problem of defending the Alamo. Its walls enclosed about three acres, and the chapel and the long barracks were in poor repair. Fewer than two hundred men could not **cover** every wall at once. The defenders added **cannon** and patched the weak spots, but it was a poor place to stand against a big army.

<!-- block 52 role=content -->
In January, Sam Houston sent Jim Bowie to San Antonio with a task. He was to look over the Alamo and decide whether it could be held. If not, he was to pull out the cannon and blow up the walls. Bowie arrived, saw the place, and decided that it was worth saving.

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
On February 2, Bowie wrote to the governor, speaking for himself and Colonel Neill, the commander. He asked for men, money, and supplies. He wrote that they would rather die in these ditches than give up the post to the enemy. It was a strong sentence, and it showed how seriously the two men took the place.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Why was San Antonio so important? Many Texians thought of it as a **picket** guard, an outpost out in front of the army. A force there could slow an invading army and give the **settlers** farther east time to prepare. It was also a fort that Texians might use as a bargaining tool, since the Mexicans would want it back.

<!-- section: Leaders and volunteers -->

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
The garrison grew a little each week. Lieutenant Colonel William B. Travis came with a few dozen soldiers, and Davy Crockett arrived with **volunteers** from Tennessee. When Colonel Neill left to care for a sick family, Travis and Bowie agreed to share command. The two men were very different, and they did not always agree.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
Not everyone inside was an American. A company of Tejanos, led by Juan **Seguín**, had joined the garrison. These Mexican-born Texans had fought for the **federal** **constitution**, and they lived in the area. For them, the Alamo was home. The defense of the Alamo was never a simple case of Americans against Mexicans.

<!-- section: Thirteen days -->

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
On February 23, 1836, **Santa Anna** 's army arrived in San Antonio. The Texians had expected a longer wait. They hurried into the Alamo and raised a flag. Santa Anna ran up a red flag from the church tower in San Antonio. It meant that no mercy would be given. The siege began.

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
Santa Anna's army was large, but it was not all in San Antonio on the first day. More soldiers arrived over the following days. The army was also tired from a hard march through winter. Santa Anna had pushed his men fast to catch the Texians by surprise, and many of his soldiers were young recruits with little training.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
On February 24, Travis wrote a letter to the people of Texas and all Americans in the world. He said that he was surrounded by a thousand or more Mexican soldiers, that he had been bombarded for twenty-four hours, and that he would never **surrender** or **retreat**. He asked for help. He signed it, "Victory or death."

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
That letter shows how Travis understood the siege. He was asking for men and he believed that help would come. Bowie fell seriously ill, and Travis commanded alone. Seguín was sent out of the fort with messages and slipped through the Mexican lines. The Texians waited, hoping that Colonel Fannin would arrive from **Goliad**.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
Fannin set out with his men and then turned back. A wagon broke, and he doubted whether he could reach the Alamo in time. On March 1, thirty-two volunteers from **Gonzales** slipped into the fort, and they were the last to arrive. After that, no more help came.

<!-- block 73 role=content -->
Think about the long days of the siege. Cannon fire fell on the walls daily. The defenders had little rest. Each morning they could see the army growing larger around them. They knew the odds. Yet they stayed, and their reasons were different. Some were fighting for Texas, some for their friends, and some for their own idea of honor.

<!-- section: The final assault -->

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
Early in the morning of March 6, Santa Anna ordered his soldiers to attack from all sides. The Texians fired as the Mexican columns came forward. The attackers reached the walls, climbed over, and opened the gates. By dawn it was over. Almost all of the defenders were dead, including Travis, Bowie, and Crockett.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
Several women, children, and an **enslaved** man named Joe were allowed to leave. One woman, Susanna Dickinson, carried the news to the Texian army. Santa Anna wanted people to know what had happened, so that other Texians would be afraid. The effect was the opposite. The story did not frighten Texians into surrender. It made them angry.

<!-- section: Independence at Washington-on-the-Brazos -->

<!-- block 79 role=content -->
During the siege, a convention was meeting in a rough building at the town of Washington-on-the-Brazos, about 150 miles away. On March 2, the delegates voted to declare independence from Mexico. Texas was no longer fighting for the Constitution of 1824. It was fighting to be a separate country.

<!-- block 80 role=content -->
Think about the delegates at Washington-on-the-Brazos. They worked in an unfinished building with no glass in the windows, in cold weather, while an army marched on their neighbors. They approved the **declaration** in a single day. They knew that it might cost them everything.

<!-- block 81 role=content -->
The Alamo's defenders almost certainly never learned of this. Messages took days to travel, and Mexican soldiers surrounded the fort. The men inside had come to defend a place, and perhaps a constitution. They died four days after independence was declared, without knowing that the war they were fighting had changed.

<!-- block 82 role=content voice -->
Because of this, historians are careful about what they say the defenders died for. We know they died for their friends, for the fort, and for the cause of Texas. We do not know what each man believed about independence. That uncertainty is part of what makes the story so human.

<!-- block 83 role=content -->
Out of the loss, a rallying cry arose. Within weeks, Texian soldiers were shouting, "Remember the Alamo!" It would help turn grief into resolve. The Alamo did not win the war. But it bought time, it cost the Mexican army many soldiers, and it showed Texians what the war was costing.
