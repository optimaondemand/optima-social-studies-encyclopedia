**Comanche** (kuh-MAN-chee) — a Native American people who became dominant on the southern Great Plains after adopting the horse, roughly 1700–1750.

<!-- section: Spain introduced the horse. Spain did not control what happened next. -->

<!-- block 7 role=content -->
After the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, horses spread rapidly out of Spanish settlements and into Native hands across the southern plains. The Comanche built a mounted way of life around them, faster and more mobile than anything the region had seen. By 1750, that power was strong enough to reverse who had the advantage on this frontier.

<!-- section: PART 1 A GIFT SPAIN COULD NOT CONTROL -->

<!-- block 16 role=content -->
Spain brought horses to North America as tools of conquest and settlement. After the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 disrupted Spanish control in New Mexico, horses spread rapidly out of Spanish hands and into Native ones. Over the following decades, roughly 1700 to 1750, the Comanche moved onto the southern plains and built a way of life organized around mounted mobility.

<!-- section: PART 2 WHAT THE HORSE MADE POSSIBLE -->

<!-- block 18 role=content -->
As Comanche power grew, the Apache, known earlier as the Querecho, were pushed south into Spanish Texas itself, and began raiding it. This was not the Apache choosing conflict for its own sake; it was displacement, a real consequence of a shifting balance of power on the plains.

<!-- block 19 role=content -->
A mounted way of life made bison hunting possible at a scale walking never allowed, gave the Comanche far greater range, and built a raiding economy no purely pedestrian society on these plains could match. This is the inversion the lesson's title names: the model Spain brought to control this frontier is what ends up arming the power that closes it.
