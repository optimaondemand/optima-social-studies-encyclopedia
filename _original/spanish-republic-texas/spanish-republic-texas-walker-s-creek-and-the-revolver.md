<!-- section: A bankrupt inventor's rejected gun finally meets its problem. -->

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
Samuel Colt patented the revolver in 1836. Almost nobody wanted it, and his company was bankrupt by 1842. The Republic of Texas Navy had bought a batch of his Paterson revolvers anyway; when the navy was laid up, those pistols passed to the Texas Rangers, and everything changed.

<!-- section: PART 1 WHY THIS HAD NEVER WORKED BEFORE -->

<!-- block 13 role=content tool_mention -->
Cavalry had tried to solve the mounted-firearms problem before. The caracole, riders firing a single shot then wheeling away to reload, was tried for roughly a century and eventually abandoned. Dragoons sometimes dismounted just to get one good shot off. In every case, giving up the mounted charge to fire a single shot was never worth the tradeoff against a continuously-reloading mounted opponent. <!-- section: PART 2 A CREEK BED, AND A REVERSAL IN POLICY -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
In June 1844, Ranger captain Jack Hays led roughly fifteen men against a far larger Comanche force at Walker's Creek, and fought the entire engagement from the saddle using Paterson revolvers. It worked. Samuel Walker later worked directly with Colt to design an improved model, the Walker Colt, in 1847; the U.S. Army ordered a thousand of them, and Colt's company, bankrupt only five years earlier, began its rise from a Texas creek bed.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW -->

<!-- block 19 role=content -->
Tonkawa scouts worked closely with the Rangers throughout this period, while the Waco were present at the Tehuacana Creek councils when Houston returned to office and reversed Lamar's policy. Indian groups on this frontier had varied, sometimes opposing relationships with Texans; there was no single, uniform position.
