<!-- section: Paper from the wrong government -->

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
In the summer of 1821, Stephen F. Austin rode into San Antonio with his father's grant in his hands. The Spanish governor welcomed him and agreed to honor it. Austin explored the land between the **Brazos** and **Colorado** rivers, chose a place for his colony, and went back to Louisiana to find **settlers**. By the end of that year, the first families were arriving.

<!-- block 45 role=content -->
But the ground had shifted under him. In September 1821, Mexico won its **independence**, and Spain no longer ruled the land where Austin's settlers were building cabins. The grant had come from the king of Spain. A new country now owned Texas, and the new country had never agreed to anything. Austin's colony had no legal footing at all.

<!-- block 46 role=content -->
Austin could have waited and hoped that someone would notice him. He chose not to. If the new government did not **confirm** the grant, his settlers could lose their land, and he would lose the money he had spent. The only place to ask was the capital. In December 1821, he left for Mexico City, about a thousand miles away.

<!-- section: A long road -->

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
The trip took months. Austin crossed deserts and mountain passes, passed through towns where almost nobody had heard of Texas, and finally reached Mexico City on April 29, 1822. He did not speak Spanish well yet. He had no friends in the government. He carried a few letters of introduction and a great deal of patience.

<!-- block 49 role=content -->
The country itself was a hard place to cross. Roads were poor, and rivers flooded in the rainy season. Travelers had to watch for robbers on the lonely stretches. A trip that takes a day or two by car today took Austin about four months. Every mile of it reminded him how far Texas was from the people who would decide its future.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
Think about how this looked from the capital. The leaders there had just won a war, and they were arguing about how to run a country. A request from an American to bring American families into Texas was a small matter next to those problems. Austin had to find a way to make his problem matter to people who already had many others.

<!-- section: An emperor in the making -->

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
Austin arrived in the middle of a political crisis. The man who had led Mexico to independence was Agustín de **Iturbide**. Iturbide had many supporters in the army, but he did not get along with the **congress**. Mexico had no king, and nobody had agreed on what kind of government the country should have.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
On the night of May 18, 1822, soldiers and a crowd marched through the streets shouting for Iturbide to become **emperor**. The next day, congress voted to make him emperor while the crowd waited outside. Austin watched all of this from the city. In July, Iturbide was crowned in a ceremony that looked a lot like the courts of Europe.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
Austin wrote letters about what he saw. He was surprised by how quickly power could shift and how much depended on friendships and favors. He could see that the new emperor's hold on the country was shaky. A leader who had won a war was learning that governing a country was a very different job.

<!-- section: Learning the game -->

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
Austin used his time well. He studied Spanish every day until he could read and write it with ease. He learned who held power in each office and who could be trusted. He paid calls on officials, waited in outer rooms, and came back again when he was told to return later. He had no other way to be heard.

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
Austin also met **Arizpe**, a priest and lawmaker from northern Mexico who had helped shape the idea of a **federal** **republic**. The two men talked about how the United States was governed. Austin translated the U.S. **Constitution** into Spanish for Arizpe. In return, Austin gained a friend who knew how Mexican lawmakers thought.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
He also had to make himself useful. Officials wanted to know about the lands in the north, and Austin was one of the few people who had walked them. He answered questions, explained how a colony might help guard the **frontier**, and wrote out his requests with care. Slowly, officials who had ignored him began to listen.

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
Austin was far from home, and the colony he had started depended on whether his trip succeeded. His settlers waited without a clear answer, and some of them grew nervous. It took a lot of steadiness to stay in a strange city with no promise that the trip would work. Many people would have given up and gone home. Austin stayed.

<!-- section: The Imperial Colonization Law -->

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
In early January 1823, Iturbide's government passed the Imperial Colonization Law. It was the first Mexican law to lay out how colonists could receive land in Texas. Austin had spent months talking with officials about what such a law should say, and many of its terms matched what he had asked for. The law offered land to families who would settle, and Austin's colony fit it well.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
The law set aside land for each farmer and each rancher, and it rewarded the person who brought in the families. It also said that settlers had to obey Mexican law and practice the Catholic faith. These were the terms that Austin would use in his colony for years to come. He had not only won a favor. He had helped to shape the rules.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
In February, the government confirmed Austin's grant under the new law. He had what he came for. His settlers would be allowed to stay, and he would be allowed to bring in more families. After nearly a year of waiting, a long trip, and many meetings, the colony stood on legal ground again.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
There was one problem. The government that had confirmed Austin's grant was about to fall. Army officers were already turning against the emperor, and by March 1823 he would be forced to give up his throne. Austin's hard-won paper was signed by a ruler who would soon be gone. What happened next shaped the future of Texas.

<!-- section: What Austin learned -->

<!-- block 72 role=content -->
Austin came home with more than a signed paper. He had learned that Mexican governments could change quickly, and that the people he knew often mattered more than the rules written on paper. A friend in the right office was worth more than a clause in a law. He had also learned how little Mexico's leaders knew about Texas.

<!-- block 73 role=content -->
These were lessons that would shape the colony for years. Austin would keep careful ties with officials in the capital and in the state government. He would keep his people calm when news from the capital was bad. He would also make this long trip a second time, and it would go far worse.
