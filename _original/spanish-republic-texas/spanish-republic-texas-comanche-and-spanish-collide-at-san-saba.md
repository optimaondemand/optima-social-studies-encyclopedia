<!-- section: Spain built a mission for one people and was attacked by another. -->

<!-- block 28 role=content -->
In 1757, Spain built a **mission** on the San Saba River for the **Apache**. A year later, it was destroyed by a large force led by the **Comanche**. The attack showed Spain how little it controlled. It also led, in the end, to a decision that Spain had never wanted to make: to give up a part of its own **province**.

<!-- section: San Sabá, 1757 -->

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
The **Lipan** Apache were under pressure from the Comanche, and some of them asked Spain for a mission. Spanish officials liked the idea. A mission would help keep the Lipan as **allies**. There were also **rumors** of silver in the hills nearby, and Spain hoped to find mines there.

<!-- block 46 role=content -->
The idea looked good from Mexico City. A mission would make the Lipan into Spanish subjects, farmers, and allies. It would also put a Spanish presence on the edge of the plains, where the Comanche ruled. Officials hoped it would open the way to more missions and, perhaps, to mines. They spent a great deal of money on the plan. They had not asked what the Comanche would think.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
In 1757, Spain built a mission on the **San Sabá** River, and a fort a few miles away. Colonel **Ortiz Parrilla** commanded the soldiers. But the plan had a flaw. The Lipan came and went, and almost none of them settled at the mission. They wanted protection from the Comanche, not a new way of life. And their enemies were watching.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
Think about what the soldiers and priests saw in the first months. Few Lipan came, and those who did came and went. The priests had few people to teach, and the soldiers had a fort to guard and no clear enemy in sight. Everyone knew the Comanche were somewhere to the north. Nobody knew when they would come.

<!-- section: A force of two thousand -->

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
In March 1758, a large force gathered around the mission. It was led by the Comanche and included allies from several other peoples, among them the **Taovayas**. The Spaniards counted about two thousand warriors. Many carried guns, and some of the guns were French.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
The attackers burned the mission and killed eight people, including two priests. The survivors ran to the fort and held out inside. The mission was never rebuilt. It was the only Spanish mission in Texas destroyed in this way.

<!-- block 56 role=content voice -->
A priest named **Molina** survived the attack and wrote an account of it. Because of him, we know what the attackers looked like and what they did. His account is one of the main sources for this story. Like any witness, he wrote from his own view, and he had good reason to fear the people who attacked him.

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
Why did they strike? The Lipan were their enemies, and Spain had just built a mission for them. To the Comanche and their allies, the mission was a base for their enemies and a threat. They did not need to fight Spain in general. They could remove a small outpost of it.

<!-- section: A Spanish army on the Red River -->

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
Spain wanted to punish the attackers. In 1759, Colonel Ortiz Parrilla led about five hundred men north to the Red River, including soldiers, Apache allies, and others. They found a Taovayas village protected by a log wall and a ditch. A flag flew over it that Spaniards believed was French.

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
The battle lasted about four hours. The defenders were well armed and well placed, and the Spaniards could not break through. Spanish soldiers began to panic. They chose to **retreat**, and they left their **cannons** behind. It was one of the worst defeats Spain ever suffered on the Texas **frontier**.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
The result shocked Spain. A Spanish army had been beaten by Native peoples defending a village with a wall, a ditch, and good weapons. News of the defeat spread, and other peoples on the frontier took note. Spain's power on the plains had always depended on a belief that its soldiers could not be beaten. That belief was broken.

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
The lesson was clear. Spanish soldiers had no good way to catch mounted warriors on open ground. And Spain could not afford to try again.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
Part of the problem was the size of Texas. A Spanish army could march a few hundred miles into the plains, but it could not keep marching. Horses tired, food ran low, and the long road home was open to attack. The Comanche, who lived on the plains, had no such problem. They could retreat, circle back, and strike again, on their own ground and on their own terms.

<!-- section: Rubí's report -->

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
Spain sent an officer named **Rubí** to inspect the whole northern frontier. From 1766 to 1768, he traveled to every fort. He found them poorly placed, poorly supplied, and spread too thin. His report recommended that Spain pull back. It should hold a shorter line of strong forts, and it should give up places it could not defend.

<!-- block 74 role=content -->
The inspector was not looking for a fight. He was looking for facts. He measured distances, counted soldiers, and asked what each fort was really worth. Many of the forts he saw protected little and cost a lot. His job was to give the king a clear picture, even if the picture was not pleasing.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
In 1772, the **Crown** issued new **regulations** that put much of his plan into effect. A year later, Spain ordered East Texas **abandoned**. **Settlers** in the East Texas missions and towns were told to leave and move to San Antonio. The Crown gave up a part of Texas that it had first claimed to protect from the French.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
This was a hard choice for Spain. East Texas had been claimed first, and the missions there were older than San Antonio. Families who had lived there for decades were told to pack up and go. Many did not want to leave. Some later returned, and their return led to the founding of the town of **Nacogdoches**. Even so, the order showed that Spain was now thinking about what it could keep, not what it could claim.

<!-- block 79 role=content -->
Geography helps explain the decision. West and north of San Antonio, the land rises and the rainfall drops. Open, drier country lies beyond. Missions that depended on farms and ditches could not live there, and neither could Spanish settlers. The edge of Spanish power roughly followed the edge of the country where farming was possible.

<!-- block 80 role=content voice imperative -->
Look at a rainfall map next to a map of Spanish Texas. The settlements, missions, and forts follow the wetter lands of the south and east. They stop where the rain stops. Spain did not draw this line on purpose. It was a line that the climate, the Comanche, and Spain's own limits drew together. Spain could cross the line with an army, but it could not stay.
