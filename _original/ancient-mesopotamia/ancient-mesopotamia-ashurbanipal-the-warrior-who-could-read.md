<!-- section: Can a warrior-king's greatest legacy be a library instead of a conquest? -->

<!-- block 11 role=content -->
Assyria's empire was built through Ashurnasirpal II's terror and Shalmaneser III's steady growth. It reached its widest borders, and its greatest work of the mind, under one final king, Ashurbanipal, who boasted of hunting lions like any warrior-king, but who could also read and write cuneiform himself and used his empire's resources to gather the ancient world's largest known library.

<!-- section: A KING WHO LEARNED TO READ -->

<!-- block 25 role=content -->
Assyrian kings were warriors first. Ashurbanipalah-shur-BAH-nih-palking of Assyria from about 668 to 627 BC, whose library at Nineveh preserved thousands of Mesopotamian texts. was no exception. He boasted in his own writings of hunting lions and leading campaigns. This was the same kind of proud royal language common in ancient royal inscriptions. Stone reliefs from his palace are among the finest carvings to survive from ancient Assyria. They show him hunting lions released from cages in a royal park. He spears and shoots them at close range, in front of a watching crowd. Scholars today often call these carvings the Lion Hunt reliefs. They were meant to show the king as Assyria's protector, brave enough to face its fiercest predator with his own hands.

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
In other words, Ashurbanipal wanted to be remembered two ways at once. He wanted to be seen as a fighter strong enough to face wild lions. He also wanted to be seen as a scholar wise enough to read the old gods' own words. But he also claims something highly unusual for a Mesopotamian king. He says he himself was taught to read and write cuneiform as a young prince. That was a skill normally left only to trained scribes. In his own words, he describes learning "the wisdom of Nabu." Nabu was the Mesopotamian god of writing and knowledge. He studied hard scholarly tablets in both Akkadian and the much older Sumerian language. By his own time, Sumerian had not been spoken aloud for well over a thousand years. Yet scribes still preserved it. They studied it and copied it, the way students today might treat a truly ancient classical language. In other words, Ashurbanipal studied a language that was, to him, already ancient history. This is much like a student today studying a language no one speaks anymore. The goal, then and now, is to unlock what it can still teach.

<!-- section: THE LIBRARY AT NINEVEH -->

<!-- block 31 role=content tool_mention -->
Ashurbanipal used his position, and his empire's resources, to gather an enormous royal librarya planned collection of written tablets or scrolls, gathered and kept in one place for study and safekeeping. at his capital, NinevehNIN-uh-vuhAshurbanipal's royal capital, on the Tigris River, home to his great library.. He sent agents throughout Assyria, and above all into Babylonia, to collect, copy, and bring back tabletsa flat piece of clay, used across Mesopotamia for writing in cuneiform, often dried or baked hard afterward.. These tablets covered every subject: medicine, astronomy, omens, grammar, royal history, and story-poems. Modern archaeologists have found many thousands of tablets from this library. Among them is a nearly whole copy of the **Epic of Gilgamesh**, the same hero tale that began in Sumerian Uruk. It survives here in its fullest ancient form because Ashurbanipal's scribes thought it worth copying and keeping. Without this library, much of what scholars today know about Sumerian and Babylonian writing might simply be lost. In other words, one king's own curiosity ended up saving stories and knowledge that might otherwise have vanished forever.

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
Ashurbanipal's tablets often end with a **colophon**, a short scribal note naming the tablet's owner. Some colophons even warn future readers against removing or damaging the tablet, calling down the gods' anger on anyone who might steal from the collection. It is a small, very human detail. Here is a king proud enough of his library to threaten curses in its defense.

<!-- block 33 role=content -->
The library sat buried and forgotten for over two thousand years. British archaeologists, Austen Henry Layard and later Hormuzd Rassam, dug into Nineveh's ruins in the 1850s. They uncovered room after room packed with broken clay tablets, tens of thousands of pieces in all. Museum staff and scholars have spent well over a century sorting, matching, and translating these pieces. New pieces are still being matched and joined together even today. In other words, a library one king built was lost for over two thousand years. It was found again, and pieced back together, by people from a country Ashurbanipal never even knew existed.

<!-- section: A WARNING BENEATH THE GLORY -->

<!-- block 38 role=content -->
Ashurbanipal's reign was not only lions and learning. Ashurbanipal's father had placed his own older brother, Shamash-shum-ukin, on the throne of Babylon as a separate, junior king. For several years, the two brothers fought a brutal civil war against each other. That war ended with Babylon besieged and Ashurbanipal's brother dead. It is worth naming plainly, since it hints at exactly the kind of danger that would later bring the empire down. Even at Assyria's proudest height, a fight within the royal family itself could shake the whole empire to its foundations. In other words, it was Ashurbanipal's own family, not some foreign enemy, that came closest to tearing his empire apart. This happened while he still sat on his own throne.

<!-- section: AN IRONY WORTH SITTING WITH -->

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
Nineveh's palace, library included, would be destroyed by fire within a generation of Ashurbanipal's death. Fire almost always destroys writing. But clay tablets are different. They do not act like papyrus or parchment. The very fire that destroyed Ashurbanipal's palace baked his unbaked clay tablets hard. In effect, it fired them like pottery. This accident helped preserve thousands of tablets, shattered but largely whole, for archaeologists to recover and piece back together more than two thousand years later. By pure accident, a disaster that ended a king's dynasty also protected some of his greatest work. History rarely hands out its ironies this cleanly, but this one is real.
