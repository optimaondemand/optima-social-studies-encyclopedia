<!-- section: A MINOR CITY RISES -->

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
Among the many small city-states an Amorite family came to rule was Babylon, a fairly small town on the Euphrates. At the start of the second millennium BC, Babylon had no special claim to greatness. That changed with the reign of Hammurabi, who took Babylon's throne around 1792 BC. For much of his reign, Hammurabi ruled a modest kingdom, careful and mostly on guard, forming and breaking deals with bigger neighbors as needed. Only late in his reign did this pattern change. In a fast series of wars during his final decade of rule, Hammurabi finally joined southern and much of central Mesopotamia under Babylon's rule, beating rival cities one after another, including Larsa, Mari, and Eshnunna, each of which had once been at least as strong as Babylon itself.

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
This timing matters. It would be easy to picture Hammurabi as a conqueror from the very start of his reign. The actual pattern was not that simple. Contingencythe idea that history could easily have turned out differently, because it depended on small, uncertain events, not one fixed path. shows up here too, just as it did with Sargon. If Hammurabi had died at fifty instead of ruling into his final years of war, Babylon might have stayed a middling kingdom, known today, if at all, as just one Amorite city-state among many.

<!-- section: MORE THAN A CONQUEROR -->

<!-- block 29 role=content -->
Hammurabi's military wins are not what makes his reign matter most to history. He is best known today for something else instead: a written code of lawa single, written collection of laws meant to apply the same way across an entire kingdom, rather than being decided case by case.. This code was carved onto a huge stone steleSTEE-leea tall, upright stone slab, often carved with writing or images, set up in a public place. and set up in public. Hammurabi's Code survives largely whole today, nearly four thousand years later, covering nearly every part of daily life: family, land, work, farming, and trade. It also clearly frames Hammurabi's rule around one central promise: justice for his subjects.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW A NOTE ON CHRONOLOGY -->

<!-- block 31 role=content -->
Historians place Hammurabi's reign at roughly 1792 to 1750 BC using the "Middle Chronology," one of several rival dating systems scholars use for the ancient Near East. A Babylonian record of the planet Venus, kept by ancient sky-watchers, is one key piece of evidence scholars compare against modern star charts to test rival systems. The Middle Chronology is used throughout, but other textbooks may use a different one.

**Hammurabi** ruled Babylon from about 1792 to 1750 BC. He spent most of his reign as a careful, guarded king, only finishing his conquest of Mesopotamia in his final years of rule. He is known today mainly for his code of law, one of the most complete and best-kept law collections to survive from the ancient world.
