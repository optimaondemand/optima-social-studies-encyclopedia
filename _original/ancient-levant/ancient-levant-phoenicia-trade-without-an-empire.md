<!-- section: CITIES, NOT AN EMPIRE -->

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
Phoeniciafuh-NEE-shuha narrow strip of coastline in the modern Levant, home to a string of independent trading cities. was never one single kingdom, the way Egypt or Assyria was. It was a group of independent city-states along the Levant coast. Each city had its own king, its own harbor, and its own fleet. They worked together when it helped them. They competed when it did not. This pattern should sound familiar. It is close to the pattern of Sumerian cities like Uruk: separate centers held together more by shared culture and trade than by any single crown. In other words, no one king ever ruled all of Phoenicia at once. Phoenicia's cities had little land and no great army. What they lacked in territory, they made up for in ships.

<!-- block 25 role=content tool_mention -->
Phoenician sailors became the finest navigators and shipbuilders anywhere on the Mediterranean Sea. Using tall, prized stands of Lebanese cedar, they built strong ships that could cross open water other peoples avoided. Phoenician traders carried glassware and fine metalwork. Above all, they carried Tyrian purpleTEER-ee-una costly purple dye Phoenicians made from crushed sea snails., a costly dye pulled drop by drop from the crushed glands of a small sea snail. This dye cost so much that its color became a mark of royalty for centuries afterward, all the way into the Roman Empire. Phoenician traders needed tin to make bronze, but had none of their own. So they pushed steadily west, planting trading posts and coloniesKOL-uh-neea settlement founded by people from another land, still linked back to its home city by trade and culture. along the North African coast, in Sicily and Sardinia, and as far as the Strait of Gibraltar and the Iberian coast beyond it. <!-- section: PEOPLES THE PHOENICIANS -->

<!-- block 28 role=content -->
The **Phoenicians** were a Semitic-speaking people of the Levant coast, organized into independent trading city-states rather than one kingdom. This relief, carved on the sarcophagus of King Ahiram of Byblos, shows Phoenician dress and courtly life. Their ships, dye, and alphabet spread across the whole Mediterranean world.

<!-- section: A DIFFERENT KIND OF GIFT -->

<!-- block 30 role=content -->
Phoenicia's most lasting gift to the wider world was not a good at all. It was a tool. Egyptian hieroglyphics and Mesopotamian cuneiform each needed scribes to master hundreds of different signs. The alphabetAL-fuh-beta writing system where each symbol stands for one sound, not a whole word or idea. the Phoenicians built and spread used only about twenty-two simple symbols. Each one stood for a single consonant sound. In other words, it was simply easier to learn. A trading network stretched across the whole Mediterranean was exactly the kind of place an easier writing system could spread fast. It moved port to port, carried by merchants, not just kept inside temple and palace walls. Within a few centuries, Greek traders picked up this same alphabet. They adapted it further, adding their own vowel signs. That adapted Greek alphabet is, eventually, the distant ancestor of the one used to write this very sentence. In other words, a system built for busy merchants counting cargo ended up shaping how nearly the whole Western world reads and writes today.

<!-- section: CITIES TYRE, SIDON, AND BYBLOS -->

<!-- block 33 role=content -->
**Tyre**, **Sidon**, and **Byblos** were Phoenicia's three leading port cities. Tyre, built partly on an island, was the wealthiest and later withstood a thirteen-year siege by Nebuchadnezzar II without being destroyed.

<!-- section: VALUABLE ENOUGH TO SPARE -->

<!-- block 35 role=content -->
Phoenicia's story took a harder turn in the sixth century BC. Babylon's king Nebuchadnezzar II, builder of the Ishtar Gate and destroyer of Jerusalem, now turned toward the coast. Starting around 585 BC, Nebuchadnezzar laid siege to Tyrethe wealthiest of the Phoenician port cities, built partly on an island just off the coast. itself, the richest of the Phoenician cities. Tyre was built partly on an island and guarded by its own strong navy. The siege dragged on for roughly thirteen years, an amazing length of time by any ancient measure. Yet Tyre was never actually destroyed the way Jerusalem was. Surviving accounts say the siege ended in a deal. Tyre accepted Babylonian overlordship and likely paid tribute, but kept its own king, its own walls, and its own fleet mostly whole. In other words, Nebuchadnezzar's army camped outside Tyre's walls for thirteen full years and still could not force the city to surrender outright.

<!-- block 36 role=content -->
This is worth pausing on, using the same question raised by Assyria's terror and Babylon's grandeur: what actually holds a state together? Phoenicia's cities never built anything close to the war machine of Assyria. They never tried to hold land the way Egypt or Babylon did either. What kept Tyre standing through a thirteen-year siege was not strength of arms. It was being too valuable to destroy. Phoenicia controlled Mediterranean shipping, shipbuilding know-how, and a trade network no conquering power wanted to burn down along with the city. In other words, a city can sometimes protect itself just by being too useful to lose. A civilization can secure its own survival through being needed just as surely as through walls and armies, and sometimes more surely still. Compare this directly with Assyria's approach. Assyria ruled by fear and lost everything within a single generation once its army faltered. Phoenicia never had a strong army to begin with, and yet it outlasted Assyria anyway.
