While the Hittites built their empire across Anatolia, two related but separate civilizations, the Minoans on Crete and the Mycenaeans on the Greek mainland, ran their own palace economies, tracking sheep, grain, and chariots on thousands of clay tablets. <!-- section: KNOSSOS AND AN UNWALLED PUZZLE -->

<!-- block 23 role=content -->
Minoan civilization is named by modern archaeologists for the legendary King Minos. It grew up on Crete from around 3000 BC. It reached its height in a palace-centered society by roughly 1700 BC. The largest Minoan palace, at **Knossos**, was an enormous, sprawling complex. It held storage rooms, workshops, and living quarters. It was also fitted with truly advanced plumbing: clay pipes, flushing latrines, and light-wells that carried sunlight deep into the building. This was among the most advanced water systems found anywhere in the Bronze Age world. Much of what visitors see at Knossos today was rebuilt in concrete in the early 1900s. The British archaeologist Sir Arthur Evans, who dug up the site, did this rebuilding himself. Evans made his best guess at how the palace once looked. Many of his choices are now seen as guesswork, not accurate fact. This is worth remembering whenever a ruined site looks a little too complete.

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
Here is Knossos's real puzzle, though, one worth sitting with rather than explaining away. Unlike nearly every other major Bronze Age capital, Knossos was built with no walls at all. Historians still debate why. Perhaps Crete's naval strength made land walls unneeded. Ships patrolling the sea around the island may have protected it instead. Perhaps Minoan society was set up in different ways than its heavily walled neighbors, with less to fear from rivals close by. The honest answer, again, is that no single reason has won out. This remains an open question, much like the missing palaces of the Indus Valley. Some historians also point to a huge volcanic eruption on the nearby island of Thera, around 1600 BC, as a blow to Minoan sea power. Even so, Knossos itself carried on for centuries after that eruption, a reminder that no single event fully explains a civilization's later fortunes.

<!-- section: CITIES KNOSSOS AND MYCENAE -->

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
**Knossos**, sprawling and unwalled, and **Mycenae**, compact and heavily fortified behind its famous Lion Gate, represent two very different answers to the same Bronze Age question: how should a palace center protect itself and its people?

<!-- block 28 role=content -->
Minoan palace officials kept records in a script called **Linear A**, still undeciphered today. Even so, its numbers, a base-ten counting system, can be read even though the words around them cannot. This is a useful reminder. A writing system can be partly understood, its numbers clear while its words stay locked away. This is worth remembering the next time an undeciphered script comes up.

<!-- section: MYCENAE AND THE WANAX -->

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
On the Greek mainland, a related but separate civilization, the **Mycenaeans**, rose to power from around 1650 BC. By around 1450 BC, they had taken over Crete and folded it into their own reach. Mycenaean palace centers stood at sites including Mycenae, Tiryns, and Pylos. Each was built around a ruler called the king or ruler🔊WAH-naks (wanax)the title for the ruler at the head of a Mycenaean palace state., at the head of a detailed chain of officials and scribes. Those scribes wrote in **Linear B**, adapted from Linear A to record an early form of the Greek language itself, the earliest known Greek writing of all. It would stay undeciphered until 1952, though. That year, the architect and self-taught language expert Michael Ventris finally cracked it. Ventris had long guessed that Linear B recorded some unknown, non-Greek language. He only changed his mind once the patterns he had charted stopped making sense any other way. He announced his surprising result in a BBC radio broadcast: the tablets were Greek after all.

<!-- block 33 role=content -->
Mostly, what Linear B tablets record are numbers: sheep, chariots, grain, and land, all counted and tracked for palace bookkeeping. This is the palace economya system in which a central palace collects, stores, and hands back out most of a region's food and goods, rather than people trading freely. in its clearest form. In this system, the palace itself collected, stored, and handed back out much of a region's farm and craft goods. Workers and goods flowed in and out of the palace's own accounts, rather than trading freely in an open market.

<!-- block 36 role=content -->
**Michael Ventris** (VEN-tris) was a British architect who, working outside any university, deciphered Linear B in 1952, proving it recorded an early form of Greek. His breakthrough remains one of the great achievements in the history of decoding ancient scripts.

<!-- section: TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT THE PALACE ECONOMY -->

<!-- block 39 role=content -->
A palace economy is a system in which a central palace, not an open market, controls most of a region's farm and craft output. Mycenaean scribes tracked sheep, grain, wine, oil, chariots, and workers on thousands of Linear B tablets, almost all of them plain bookkeeping rather than poetry or history. Goods flowed into the palace as tribute or tax, were stored in huge palace warehouses, and flowed back out again as rations, tools, or raw material for palace workshops. This same basic pattern, a central authority collecting and redistributing goods rather than leaving trade to individuals, first appeared with Sumerian temples, and it would reappear in the Persian Empire's own vast bureaucracy.

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
Unlike unwalled Knossos, Mycenae itself was heavily fortified. It was protected by massive **Cyclopean walls**, built from stones so large that later Greeks could not picture ordinary humans lifting them. Instead, they credited the building of these walls to the mythical one-eyed Cyclopes. Mycenaean elite burial used grand tholos tombsTHOH-lohsa beehive-shaped stone burial chamber built for Mycenaean royal families.: beehive-shaped stone chambers built to hold the bodies and grave goods of Mycenae's ruling families. These grand buildings for the dead matched the grandeur of the palace itself in life.

<!-- section: PEOPLES THE MINOANS AND MYCENAEANS -->

<!-- block 43 role=content -->
Minoan frescoes show men in short kilts and women in flounced, layered skirts with open bodices, along with elaborate curled hairstyles. Mycenaean warriors, by contrast, are shown in bronze armor and boar's-tusk helmets, a style of dress that turns up in Homer's poems centuries later.

<!-- section: A WAR AT THE EDGE OF MEMORY -->

<!-- block 45 role=content tool_mention -->
Greek tradition remembered a great war fought by a group of Mycenaean kings against the city of Troy. The ancient scholar Eratosthenes later dated it to around 1184 BC. Homer's Iliad includes a passage known as the Catalogue of Ships, which lists the ships and forces that tradition says sailed to fight there. Like Gilgamesh's Uruk or Yu the Great's China, the Trojan War likely reflects some real kernel of Late Bronze Age memory. But the exact cast of characters, the true date, and the real scale of any such conflict remain still debated rather than confirmed history.
