<!-- section: A promise on paper -->

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
When Iturbide and the rebel leaders joined forces in 1821, they made a plan. Mexico would be independent, the Catholic Church would keep its place, and Spaniards and Mexicans would be treated as equals. The new country would be ruled by a king under written laws. The plan sounded fair to nearly everyone, and that is why it worked.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
The plan had a weak spot. It said that a Spanish prince would be invited to become king of Mexico. Spain had no interest in the offer, and the Spanish king refused to let any prince go. Mexico was left with a plan that called for a king and had no king to fill the job.

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
Think about how unusual the setting was. Mexico had been ruled by Spain for three hundred years. Hardly anyone alive had ever voted for a ruler or served in a national government. The people in **congress** were learning how to govern while they were doing it, and mistakes came quickly.

<!-- block 49 role=content -->
A temporary government took charge, and a congress met in Mexico City in early 1822 to write a **constitution**. The members of congress came from many **regions** and had many ideas. Some wanted a strong king. Some wanted a **republic**. Some wanted the army kept small. They argued for months, and the country waited.

<!-- section: A hero with no job -->

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
Iturbide was the most popular man in Mexico. He had won the war with Spain, and many soldiers and ordinary people loved him. But popularity is not the same as a plan for governing. Iturbide wanted a strong leader who could act quickly. Congress wanted to keep the power to make laws. The two sides began to distrust each other.

<!-- block 52 role=content -->
Meanwhile the country's money was running out. The war had damaged farms and mines, and the new government had few ways to collect taxes. It owed money to the army. Soldiers who are not paid grow restless, and restless soldiers look for someone to follow. In 1822, many of them looked to Iturbide.

<!-- section: The night of May 18 -->

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
On the night of May 18, 1822, a sergeant in Mexico City led a crowd of soldiers through the streets shouting that Iturbide should be emperor. The crowd grew. Iturbide acted surprised, though many people at the time doubted that he was. The next day, congress met with the crowd outside and voted to make him emperor.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
Iturbide took the throne as Agustín the First. In July, he was crowned in the great cathedral in a ceremony meant to look as grand as any in Europe. A Mexican emperor was an odd idea for a country that had just fought a war for freedom. But for a short time, it seemed to make the army and the crowds happy.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
The new emperor soon found that he had the same problems as the old congress, and he no longer had anyone to blame. The **treasury** was empty. Congress still argued with him. In October, Iturbide shut the congress down by force and replaced it with a smaller group of men he picked himself. Now almost nobody could say that he governed by law.

<!-- section: The army turns -->

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
A young army officer named **Santa Anna** made the next move. He commanded the port of **Veracruz** and had once supported Iturbide. In December 1822, he turned against him and called for a republic. Santa Anna would soon become the most powerful and most troublesome man in Mexico, and this was his first big step.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
Other officers joined him. In February 1823, a group of generals signed a plan at **Casa Mata**. It called for a new congress and did not attack the emperor directly, but everyone understood what it meant. **Province** after province backed it. Iturbide's army, the only thing holding up his throne, was leaving him.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
Iturbide tried to save himself by calling congress back in early March. It was too late. On March 19, 1823, he gave up the throne in a letter to congress. In May, he sailed into **exile** in Europe. The **empire** had lasted about ten months, and nobody had planned for it to end this way.

<!-- section: A grant approved twice -->

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
Stephen F. Austin was in the capital through all of this. Iturbide's government had just confirmed his grant, and now Iturbide was gone. Austin had to start again. The new leaders were a committee of three generals, and they looked at the grant carefully.

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
In April 1823, the new government agreed to honor Austin's grant. They did so because Austin had spent a year earning respect, because the plan to put **settlers** in Texas still made sense, and because several officials now knew him by name. A grant approved by one government, and then approved again by the one that replaced it, was much stronger than it had been before.

<!-- block 72 role=content -->
Austin's **colony** survived the fall of an empire because he had kept up good working relationships with the people who held power. He had also learned how thin the threads were that held a government together.

<!-- block 73 role=content -->
The Texas colony, meanwhile, kept on growing. Families cleared land, planted corn, and built cabins along the rivers. Most of them knew little about the fall of an emperor far away. Their daily lives depended on a government they had never seen, and on a man who was working hard to keep it friendly.

<!-- section: Lessons of a failed empire -->

<!-- block 76 role=content voice imperative -->
Notice also that none of it was settled in advance. If Iturbide had listened to congress, or if the army had stayed loyal, Mexico might have had a steadier government. Because it did not, every later leader had to deal with a country where power was often decided by who commanded the soldiers.

<!-- block 77 role=content tool_mention -->
Austin took away a simple lesson. Mexican governments changed often, and the people who held office mattered more than the paper they signed. He would have to prove this lesson again, in the capital and in Texas, many times over the years that followed.
