<!-- section: Was Texas the only part of Mexico that rose against its government? -->

<!-- block 35 role=content tool_mention voice -->
When you read about the Texas Revolution, it can seem as if Texas stood alone. It did not. In the 1830s, one **province** after another rose against the government in Mexico City, because that government had thrown away the **federal** **constitution**. This lesson shows the whole map, and where Texas fits on it.

<!-- section: Reform and reaction -->

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
In 1833, Mexico's new government began a program of **reforms**. The vice president, **Gómez Farías**, led the effort. The reforms cut back the privileges of the Church and the army, and they tried to open public life to new ideas. **Liberals** in Mexico cheered. Many others were alarmed.

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
The Church, the army, and many wealthy landowners pushed back. They saw the reforms as an attack on the oldest institutions in the country. **Conservatives** wanted Mexico to move slowly and to keep its traditions. Protests spread, and the country divided into two camps that did not trust each other.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Think about why the reforms felt so threatening. The Church owned much land and ran schools and hospitals. The army had its own courts and privileges. Taking those away would change daily life for thousands of families. People who feel that their way of life is under attack will often follow any leader who promises to protect it.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
Santa Anna had been elected as a friend of the liberals. But he was a man who followed power. In 1834, he saw that the conservatives were stronger, and he changed sides. He returned to the capital, dismissed the vice president, and sent the **congress** home. Many of the reforms were undone in a few months.

<!-- section: A strongman steps forward -->

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
Santa Anna fits a pattern that Mexico knew well in this period, the strongman (**caudillo**). A caudillo was a leader who ruled through the loyalty of his soldiers and followers rather than through laws and offices. Santa Anna called himself a defender of the nation. But he often acted as if he were the nation.

<!-- block 61 role=content tool_mention -->
Santa Anna was not simply a villain. He was brave, quick, and a skilled organizer of troops. He could also be generous when it suited him. But he lacked patience for laws that limited him, and he was willing to change his stated beliefs when his interests changed.

<!-- section: Centralists take charge -->

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
With Santa Anna's support, a new group of **centralists** took charge of the government. A centralist is someone who wants power held in the national capital. They believed that the states had too much freedom and that only a strong center could hold Mexico together. They had many friends in the army and the Church.

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
The centralists had reasons that sounded sensible to them. Mexico faced **rebellions**, an empty treasury, and the threat of **invasion** from abroad. They argued that a nation that could not act as one would not survive. Their mistake was to think that force could take the place of the consent of the provinces.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
In 1835 and 1836, the centralists wrote a new constitution called the Seven Laws. It replaced the federal constitution of 1824. The states were turned into **departments** run from the capital, and their governors were chosen by the president. The power that the states had held for a dozen years was gone.

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
To many Mexicans, this was a betrayal of the compact that had made the country. The states had joined a federal **republic**. Now the republic was being swapped for something else without their consent. People in the provinces felt that the government in the capital had broken the agreement first.

<!-- section: The lesson of Zacatecas -->

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
The state of Zacatecas was one of the richest in Mexico, with silver mines and a strong state **militia**. When the new rules limited the size of state militias, Zacatecas refused to obey. Its leaders believed that they were defending the constitution, and they raised troops.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
In May 1835, Santa Anna marched against them and won a quick victory. His soldiers then looted the city of Zacatecas. The message for other states was plain. Resistance would be crushed, and a federal system with armed states was no longer allowed.

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
Now Santa Anna had a victorious army and a free hand. The next trouble spot was to the north. In Texas, **settlers** who had been pressing for local rights were now outraged, and Santa Anna's government sent soldiers to the coast and the border. The path from Zacatecas to Texas was a direct one.

<!-- section: One province after another -->

<!-- block 74 role=content -->
Zacatecas was only the beginning. In 1835, Texas rose. In 1836, California declared itself a free state within Mexico. In 1837, rebels in New Mexico killed their governor and took over the capital before the army arrived. In 1839, **Yucatán** rebelled, and in 1840, three northern states joined in the short-lived Republic of the **Rio Grande**.

<!-- block 79 role=content -->
Texas was not unique, and its people did not yet believe that it was. Most Texans who took up arms in 1835 said that they were defending the constitution of 1824, as the people of Zacatecas had. They saw themselves as part of a much larger quarrel over how Mexico should be governed.

<!-- section: Which ones got away? -->

<!-- block 81 role=content -->
Most of these revolts failed. The army put down the rebellion in New Mexico. The Republic of the Rio Grande fell in less than a year. California's revolt ended in a deal with the government. In each case, the rebels were either defeated or won back their place in Mexico.

<!-- block 82 role=content voice imperative -->
Notice a difference between the cases. California, New Mexico, and the Rio Grande states rose to win back a federal system. Yucatán and Texas, in the end, chose to leave. A fight to restore a constitution is very different from a fight to separate. Texas would travel from the first kind of fight to the second.

<!-- block 83 role=content -->
Only two provinces actually broke away. Yucatán declared **independence** for a time, but it rejoined Mexico in 1848. Texas broke away and did not come back. Out of all the revolts, it was the only one that stayed gone.

<!-- block 84 role=content -->
Why did Texas succeed where the others failed? Part of the answer is distance, since Texas was far from the center of Mexican power. Part is the colonists' ties to the United States, which sent **volunteers** and money. And part is the luck of one afternoon in April 1836, when a small army won an unlikely battle. None of it was certain in 1835.
