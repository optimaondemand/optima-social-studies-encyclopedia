<!-- section: Can a real civilization run its cities without a king at all? -->

<!-- block 12 role=content tool_mention -->
The Indus Valley grew up far from Mesopotamia and Egypt, with little or no contact with either. This lesson meets the Indus Valley civilization: a river valley people with planned cities, standardized weights, and long-distance trade, but no palace, no royal tomb, and no writing that names a single king. That absence is a genuine, unsolved puzzle, not a gap this lesson will pretend to fill in.

<!-- section: A CIVILIZATION BUILT ON A RIVER, LIKE THE OTHERS -->

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
Farming reached the region around the Indus Rivera long river flowing through modern Pakistan, along whose banks the Indus Valley civilization grew. on its own. Wheat, barley, and a local kind of cattle called the zebu were tamed by around 7000 BC. This happened at sites like MehrgarhMER-garan early farming site in the hills near the Indus plain, where wheat, barley, and cattle were first tamed in this region., in the hill country near the river plain. Over the next centuries, this early farming culture grew into a full urban civilization. It reached its height in what historians call the **Mature Harappan** period, roughly 2600 to 1900 BC. This period is named for Harappahuh-RAP-uhthe other of the two largest Indus cities, which gave the whole civilization its name., one of its two greatest cities. The other was Mohenjo-daromoh-HEN-joh-DAR-ohone of the two largest Indus cities, known for its careful street planning and covered drains.. In 1924, the British archaeologist Sir John Marshall told the world about this whole lost civilization for the first time. Marshall's own dig report describes what his team found at Mohenjo-daro. Long before Marshall's find, British railway engineers in the 1850s had already dug up ancient bricks at Harappa. Not knowing what they had found, the engineers crushed thousands of these bricks to use as gravel under new railway tracks, wrecking part of the very civilization Marshall would later announce to the world.

<!-- section: REMARKABLY PLANNED CITIES -->

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
What Marshall's digs turned up was startling. Cities were laid out on a precise grid of straight streets. Builders used bricks cut to standard sizes across the whole civilization's land, an area larger than either Mesopotamia or Egypt at the time. Traders also used a shared system of standardized weightsa matched set of weights, built to the same sizes everywhere, used to measure goods fairly across a wide territory.. These were small cube-shaped stones, cut to matching sizes. A trader could use them to measure goods fairly, no matter where in the civilization they traveled. Mohenjo-daro's houses linked to covered brick drains running beneath the streets. Many houses even had private wells and what appear to be early bathing rooms. This level of city planning and clean water would not be matched again in much of the world for centuries.

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
At the port city of LothalLOH-tuhlan Indus port city with what may be one of the world's earliest tidal dockyards., archaeologists found what may be one of the earliest tidal dockyards built anywhere. This is proof of long-distance sea trade. Indus goods reached as far as Mesopotamia itself, where Sumerian texts speak of a trading partner called "Meluhha," widely thought to be the Indus civilization. Carved stone seals from Indus cities often show animals such as bulls, elephants, and a one-horned creature some call a "unicorn" figure. What these images meant to the people who made them is still unclear.

<!-- section: PEOPLES THE HARAPPANS -->

<!-- block 30 role=content -->
Small clay figurines and carved seals give the best clues to how the Harappans looked and dressed: draped cloth garments, elaborate hairstyles, and layered bead and shell jewelry. This famous small bronze figure, nicknamed the "Dancing Girl," shows bangles worn stacked up one arm, a style some in the region still wear today.

<!-- section: A GENUINE, UNRESOLVED PUZZLE -->

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
Here is where the Indus Valley becomes truly strange. It is stranger than the civilizations of Mesopotamia and Egypt. Despite years of careful digging, archaeologists have never found a royal palace anywhere in Indus territory. They have never found a grand temple or a huge royal tomb either. No writing naming a king, a dynasty, or even a single ruler has ever turned up. The Indus scriptthe still-undeciphered writing system found on small carved seals and pottery across the Indus Valley civilization. itself, found on small carved seals and pottery, remains undeciphered to this day. About four hundred distinct symbols have been found so far, usually written from right to left, but the language hidden behind them is still unknown. Scholars still argue over whether it even records a full spoken language. It might instead be something closer to a system of symbols and marks. Very little proof of warfare has turned up either: no walls built against other Indus cities, and no stockpiled weapons.

<!-- block 33 role=content tool_mention -->
This lesson will not pretend to solve what expert archaeologists themselves have not solved. Some historians suggest Indus cities were ruled by some kind of shared body, a council of merchants or priests, rather than a single king. Others suspect proof of kingship simply has not survived, or has not yet been found. Still others wonder if Indus society worked by rules quite different from the kingdoms and empires of Mesopotamia and Egypt, with no clean match among its Bronze Age neighbors. The honest answer, at this point in the story, is that nobody knows for sure. A good historian says so plainly, rather than making up a confident answer the evidence does not support. This mature phase of Indus civilization also faded out around 1900 BC. Many experts link this decline to a shifting river system. A once-major river likely dried up over time and left some cities without the water they needed. Even here, though, scholars have not settled on one single cause.

Mohenjo-daromoh-HEN-joh-DAR-ohone of the two largest Indus cities, known for its careful street planning and covered drains. was built on a precise grid, with wide main streets and narrower side lanes. Its most famous structure, the Great Bath shown here, was a large, carefully waterproofed pool, likely used for ritual bathing rather than everyday washing.

<!-- section: WHY THE PUZZLE MATTERS -->

<!-- block 38 role=content -->
This is worth taking seriously as its own kind of lesson. It would be easy to assume every ancient civilization must have looked something like Sumer or Egypt: a king, a temple, a monumental tomb. The Indus Valley shows that civilization does not always need the same political shape everywhere it shows up. Cities, planned streets and drains, workers with special skills, and long-distance trade can all exist without a king at their head. Keep this open question in mind when comparing it with Shang China, a river valley civilization that, unlike the Indus, left plenty of written proof naming its kings by name.
