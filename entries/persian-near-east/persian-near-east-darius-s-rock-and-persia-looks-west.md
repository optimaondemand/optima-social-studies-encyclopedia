<!-- section: A CONTESTED THRONE -->

<!-- block 25 role=content -->
According to Darius's own inscription, a man claiming to be Cambyses's brother, Bardiya, seized the Persian throne while Cambyses was still in Egypt. Darius's account insists this man was actually a fake, an impostor named Gaumata. The real Bardiya, Darius claims, had secretly been killed by Cambyses's own order years earlier. Darius killed the pretender and took the throne for himself. He then spent much of his first year as king putting down a wave of revolts across the empire. An empire this large, stitched together so quickly under Cyrus and Cambyses, was never fully settled the moment a new king took the throne. Darius had to prove, by force, that it truly answered to him.

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
There is a real limit here. Darius's version is the only full account of these events to survive. It was carved at his own command, to defend his own right to rule. No separate Persian or Egyptian record exists to check his claims against. This does not mean Darius made up the whole story. It does mean a king's own defense of seizing his own throne cannot simply be taken as plain, checked fact. The Cyrus Cylinder's words cannot be taken at face value either, for the exact same reason. A king writing about his own rise to power is never a neutral witness to it.

<!-- section: A ROCK THAT UNLOCKED AN ENTIRE FIELD -->

<!-- block 31 role=content -->
The inscription itself is known as the Behistun Inscription. It is carved high into a cliff face at Mount Behistun, in what is now western Iran. Beside the text sits a large relief image. It shows Darius standing over the defeated pretender and a line of other captured rebel leaders. What makes this inscription truly remarkable is not just its content, but its form. The same text appears three times, carved in three different languages and cuneiform scripts. This kind of carving is called a trilingual inscriptiontry-LING-gwula single carved text written out in three different languages side by side.. In other words, one single message, carved three separate times side by side, so that no one who could read any one of the three languages would be left out. Its three versions are written in **Old Persian**, **Elamite**, and Babylonian, the same script used in the Epic of Gilgamesh, Hammurabi's Code, and Ashurbanipal's library tablets.

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
Because the same message appeared in three known and unknown scripts side by side, the Behistun Inscription became cuneiform's own key to deciphermentdee-SY-fur-mentthe work of figuring out how to read a lost or unknown system of writing., in much the way the Rosetta Stone had already done for Egyptian hieroglyphics. In the 1830s and 1840s, a British officer and scholar named Henry RawlinsonRAW-lin-sunthe British scholar who copied the Behistun Inscription at great personal risk and helped decipher Babylonian cuneiform in the 1830s and 1840s. again and again risked his own life climbing the steep, dangerous cliff face to copy the inscription by hand, section by section. Using the Old Persian part, already partly known, as a starting point, Rawlinson and other scholars slowly worked out how to read Babylonian cuneiform too. This is worth stating plainly, as a closing thought. Every cuneiform source, from Sumer onward, can be read today mostly because of the work this single inscription made possible.

<!-- section: ONE EMPIRE, THREE RIVER VALLEYS -->

<!-- block 37 role=content -->
With Egypt conquered and Darius's throne now secure, the story has reached a genuine turning point. For the first time in this long story, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Persia's own homeland all answer to one empire. That is an achievement no earlier power ever managed. Not Sargon. Not Hammurabi. Not even Assyria, at its height. And this empire does not stop looking outward. The Ionian Greek cities along Anatolia's coast have been Persian subjects since Cyrus's conquest of Lydia. Just across the Aegean Sea lie other Greek cities, the mainland city-states. Those cities are about to matter a great deal.
