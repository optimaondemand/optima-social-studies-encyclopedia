**Sargon** 🔊SAR-gonfounder of the Akkadian Empire, the first empire in recorded history, ruling from roughly 2334 to 2279 BC. is this lesson's central figure, founder of the world's first known empire.

<!-- section: AN IMPROBABLE BEGINNING -->

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
According to the Sargon Birth Legend, Sargon was born to a priestess who could not openly raise him. She placed her infant son in a basket sealed with tar and set him adrift on the Euphrates River. The basket floated downstream until a man named Akki, a drawer of water, found it. Akki rescued the child and raised him as his own son, later training him as a gardener. From this humble, obscure beginning, the story says, the goddess Ishtar came to favor young Sargon. He rose to become cupbearer, a position of real trust and access, to the king of the city of Kish. From cupbearer, Sargon eventually seized power for himself. He then began the conquests that built the Akkadian Empire and founded his own royal dynastyDY-nuh-steea line of rulers from the same family, passing power down from parent to child..

<!-- block 27 role=content tool_mention voice -->
Whether every detail of this story happened exactly as told is hard to check for sure. No other written record from Sargon's own lifetime lives on to check the story against. Here is what matters for how you think about history: the entire empire that follows rests on a chain of events that did not have to happen. What if the basket had sunk? What if Akki had not been drawing water at that exact bend of the river that day? What if Sargon had never caught the king's eye? If any one of these small things had gone differently, none of the rest of this lesson would exist to tell. This is what historians mean by contingencykun-TIN-jen-seethe idea that history could easily have turned out differently, because it depended on small, uncertain events, not one fixed path.: the real past was not marching toward Sargon's empire like some fixed endpoint it had to reach. A single unlikely childhood, real or made up, stands at the root of the first empire in recorded history.

<!-- section: FROM CUPBEARER TO CONQUEROR -->

<!-- block 29 role=content -->
Once in power, Sargon moved with real urgency. He conquered the Sumerian city-states one by one, including Uruk. He defeated Uruk's king, Lugalzagesi, and brought the whole of southern Mesopotamia under one rule for the first time. He then pushed his conquests northward and westward, as far as the Mediterranean coast, and into parts of Anatolia and Elam. Sargon founded a new capital city, Akkad. Its exact spot has never been found again by archaeologists, a small but telling reminder: even a capital city ruling an empire can vanish from the map while still living on in written memory.

<!-- section: WHY "EMPIRE" IS A NEW KIND OF WORD -->

<!-- block 31 role=content -->
It is worth pausing on why historians save the word empire for what Sargon built, rather than just calling it a large kingdom. A kingdom mostly rules one people, in one place, under one set of customs. An empire rules many different peoples, cities, and languages at once. These peoples did not always choose to be ruled together, and often kept their own local customs, gods, and leaders, even while bowing to one distant ruler. Sargon had no telephones, no fast roads, and no trained government passed down from anyone before him, since no one had ever built one this large.

<!-- section: HISTORICAL FIGURE SARGON OF AKKAD -->

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
**Sargon** rose from a humble, disputed birth to found the world's first known empire, ruling Mesopotamia and beyond from his capital at Akkad around 2334 to 2279 BC. His conquests united dozens of independent Sumerian city-states under a single ruler for the first time in recorded history, setting a pattern that later empires across the ancient world would imitate.
