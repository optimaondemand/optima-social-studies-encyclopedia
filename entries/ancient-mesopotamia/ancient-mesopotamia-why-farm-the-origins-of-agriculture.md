<!-- section: Was farming really an obvious improvement over foraging? -->

<!-- block 18 role=content voice -->
Ten thousand years ago, almost every person on Earth lived the same way. Small groups of families moved with the seasons. They followed herds of wild animals. They gathered wild plants as they ripened. These families knew their land well. They knew which valley grew wild wheat in early summer. They knew which hillside sheltered wild goats in winter. This was not a bad way to live. Studies of hunter-gatherer groups who survived into modern times show something surprising. Many of these foragers ate a varied diet. Many worked fewer hours than farmers later would. Many suffered less from certain diseases. Keep this in mind as you read on. It is tempting to think that farming was simply a better idea that someone finally had. The real story is stranger and slower than that.

<!-- section: THE FERTILE CRESCENT -->

<!-- block 29 role=content -->
The story of farming begins in the Fertile CrescentFUR-tile KRESS-enta curved strip of hill country in the Middle East. Wild wheat, barley, sheep, and goats all lived here close together, which made it a good place for farming to begin.. Most of this land is rocky hill country, not flat, easy-to-farm plains. But it happened to hold wild ancestors of wheat, barley, lentils, and chickpeas. It also held wild sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs, all living in the same general area. No other place on Earth held so many useful wild plants and animals so close together.

<!-- block 30 role=content -->
Around 9500 BC, people in this region started doing something new. Instead of only gathering wild grain, they began planting seeds on purpose. They chose seeds from the plants they liked best: plants with larger seeds, and stalks that held their grain instead of scattering it before harvest. Over many centuries of this careful replanting, wild wheat and barley slowly changed into true farmed crops. By about 8500 BC, farmers across the region grew domesticatedduh-mess-tih-KAY-shunthe slow process of breeding wild plants and animals so people can grow, raise, and use them more easily. wheat and barley. Sheep and goats were domesticated soon after, by about 8000 BC. Cattle and pigs followed close behind.

<!-- block 31 role=content -->
This whole shift has a name: the Neolithic Revolutionnee-oh-LITH-ik rev-oh-LOO-shunthe slow shift, starting around 9500 BC, from hunting and gathering wild food to farming and raising it instead.. The word "revolution" might make it sound fast, but it was not. This change took place over dozens of generations. In most places, hunting and early farming existed side by side for a very long time before farming finally won out.

<!-- section: TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT FARMING AND ANIMAL DOMESTICATION -->

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
Farming did not appear overnight, and it did not appear as one single invention. It grew out of thousands of small, repeated choices. Each year, a family gathering wild grain would notice which stalks held onto their seeds longest, or which plants grew the biggest heads of grain. If they replanted seeds from those plants instead of eating them all, the next year's crop would inherit those same useful traits. Repeated over centuries, this simple habit turned wild einkorn wheat, emmer wheat, and barley into true domesticated crops that depended on people to reproduce successfully. Animal domestication followed a similar path. Herders who kept the calmest, most manageable sheep and goats alive longer, instead of hunting them at random, gradually bred animals that tolerated close contact with people and stayed near camp. Domestication changed both farmer and farmed: wheat lost its ability to scatter its own seed in the wind, and goats lost some of their wild caution. Irrigation, carrying river or spring water to fields that would not otherwise get enough rain, came later but built on the same idea: managing nature deliberately, instead of only taking what it happened to offer. Both breakthroughs made surplus food possible, and surplus made everything that follows possible.

<!-- section: A REAL TRADE-OFF, NOT AN OBVIOUS UPGRADE -->

<!-- block 36 role=content voice -->
Why does this matter for how you think about history? It would be easy to look back and assume farming was an obvious improvement. Any sensible person would choose it, right? The evidence tells a more complicated story. Skeletons from early farming villages often show harder physical labor. They show more tooth decay, likely from a grain-heavy diet. They show more disease, probably made worse by living in permanent villages next to farm animals and their waste. A forager who got sick could move away from the sickness. A farmer could not. Farmers were tied to fields that took a whole growing season to produce a harvest.

<!-- block 37 role=content -->
So why did farming spread anyway? Part of the answer is surplusSUR-plusmore food or goods than a family needs right away.. Farming can produce more food than a family needs right now, something foraging usually cannot do reliably. Surplus changes everything that follows. A family with surplus grain can support someone who does not spend the whole day finding food: a potter, a builder, eventually a priest or a soldier. Surplus stored away can also carry a village through a bad season that would have scattered a foraging band. And a settled, farming population tends to raise more children to adulthood, since babies no longer travel on long seasonal journeys. Over many generations, farming populations simply grew larger than foraging ones living in the same land.

<!-- block 38 role=content -->
This settled way of life is called sedentismSED-en-tiz-umthe practice of staying in one place all year, instead of moving from camp to camp., staying in one place year-round. Sedentism is the quiet engine behind everything that follows. Villages, cities, kings, writing, and armies all depend on people staying in one place long enough to build them.
