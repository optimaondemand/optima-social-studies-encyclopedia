<!-- section: How do we know what happened before anyone wrote anything down? -->

<!-- block 12 role=content -->
Farming spread across the Fertile Crescent thousands of years before anyone invented writing. No one alive today watched any of it happen, and no ancient author left behind a diary of the Neolithic Revolution. So how do historians know it happened at all? The answer comes from patient digging, not old books. Two real excavations, one at Jericho and one at Çatalhöyük, show exactly how archaeologists turn buried dirt, stone, and bone into real historical knowledge.

<!-- section: JERICHO: WALLS BEFORE POTTERY -->

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
One of the greatest tells anywhere sits at Jericho, near the Jordan River in the region called the Levant. In the early 1950s, the British archaeologist **Kathleen Kenyon** dug deep into Jericho's mound. She found something startling: a massive stone tower, more than eight meters tall, attached to a thick stone wall. The wall and tower date to roughly 8000 BC. This makes Jericho one of the oldest walled settlements ever found, built before people had even invented pottery.

<!-- block 25 role=content voice -->
Why build a monument like this so early? This was before writing, before the wheel, before fired clay pots. Kenyon herself argued over the answer. A wall to keep out raiders is the obvious guess. But some archaeologists think the wall might have held back flash floods or mudslides instead. Others think the tower had some special role in Jericho's rituals, a role we do not fully grasp today. This is worth sitting with honestly. Sometimes the physical evidence is clear, but the human reason behind it is not. That gap, between what we can prove and what we can only guess, is part of what makes history a subject built on careful reasoning. It is not simple fact-finding.

<!-- section: CITIES JERICHO -->

<!-- block 28 role=content -->
Picture a walled settlement of simple mudbrick and stone houses clustered around a single massive stone tower built into the settlement's thick outer wall. No streets ran in straight lines. Paths wound between houses built close together, with fields of wheat and barley just outside the wall, watered by a nearby freshwater spring.

<!-- section: ÇATALHÖYÜK: A TOWN WITHOUT STREETS -->

<!-- block 33 role=content -->
About a thousand years later, several hundred kilometers north, a very new kind of Neolithic town grew up at Çatalhöyük, in what is now central Turkey. The British archaeologist **James Mellaart** began digging there in the 1960s. He found a town with no streets at all. Its mudbrick houses were built wall to wall, packed together like a honeycomb. Residents did not enter their homes through a front door. Instead, they climbed down a ladder through a hole in the roof, walking across the rooftops of the whole town to get from house to house.

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
Inside many homes, Mellaart's team found painted walls showing hunting scenes. They also found startling **bucrania**, the horned skulls of wild cattle, set into walls and platforms. Archaeologists still argue over exactly what these skulls meant to the people who placed them there, though most agree they held some ritual or family meaning.

<!-- section: CITIES ÇATALHÖYÜK -->

<!-- block 37 role=content -->
Imagine a town with no streets or doors at ground level at all. Flat-roofed mudbrick houses packed together wall to wall, so closely that people walked across the rooftops to get anywhere, climbing down ladders through roof holes into rooms with wall paintings and the horned skulls of wild cattle.

Mellaart's finds remain central to how historians picture daily life in an early farming town.

<!-- section: WHAT A REPORT CAN AND CANNOT TELL YOU -->

<!-- block 42 role=content voice -->
Here is an important idea for how to read sources. Kenyon's and Mellaart's excavation reports are **primary sources**, but only about the dig itself. What Kenyon wrote is a firsthand, eyewitness account of what her team actually found in the ground at Jericho in the 1950s. That much is primary evidence, the direct word of someone who was there. But neither Kenyon nor Mellaart lived during the Neolithic period they were digging up. Their reports are **secondary evidence** about the Neolithic itself. They are careful, expert readings of physical clues, not eyewitness memories of Neolithic life. Ask yourself this question about any source you read: is this person a firsthand witness to the event I actually care about? Or is this person an expert explaining evidence about it long after the fact?

<!-- block 43 role=content -->
Jericho sits at roughly 31.9 degrees north latitude and 35.4 degrees east longitude, near the Jordan River valley. Çatalhöyük sits much farther north and slightly west, at roughly 37.7 degrees north latitude and 32.8 degrees east longitude, on the Anatolian plateau. Take a moment with a world map or atlas and plot both points. Notice how far apart they are, and yet how similar their basic story is. In two separate corners of the Fertile Crescent's wider neighborhood, small bands of people chose to settle down for good, within about a thousand years of each other, without ever meeting. That is not just luck, and it is not the work of two clever people acting alone. It is what happens when a deeper shift, the slow domestication of plants and animals, finally reaches a tipping point.
