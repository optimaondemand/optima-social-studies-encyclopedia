<!-- section: DIDO AND THE NEW CITY -->

<!-- block 26 role=content -->
Carthage was the greatest of the Phoenician colonies. It was planted on the North African coast, not far from modern Tunis. Its own name, in Phoenician, meant simply "New City." The oldest surviving version of its founding story comes from a later writer named Justin. In it, a Tyrian princess named DidoDY-doha Tyrian princess said to have fled Phoenicia and founded Carthage around 814 BC., sometimes called Elissa, fled her home city. Her own brother, King Pygmalion of Tyre, had killed her husband for his wealth. Dido sailed west with a band of loyal followers. She struck a shrewd, tricky deal with the local Berber ruler over how much land her new settlement could claim. Through that deal, she founded Carthage on the North African shore. Old tradition dates this founding to 814 BC.

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
Centuries later, the Roman poet Virgil told a very different version of Dido's story. His epic, the *Aeneid*, was written under the emperor Augustus. In Virgil's telling, Dido falls in love with the Trojan hero Aeneas, blown ashore at Carthage while fleeing the fall of Troy. She kills herself in despair when he sails on toward Italy to found the line that will one day become Rome. This version is fine writing, but it cannot be real history, and that should be said plainly. The Trojan War, whatever its real kernel, belongs to an age centuries before 814 BC. That is the very Bronze Age that had collapsed centuries before. Virgil knew this well. He was not making a mistake. He was writing centuries after Rome and Carthage had already fought their real wars. He used this anachronismuh-NAK-ruh-niz-uma detail placed in the wrong time period, out of its real historical order., a detail placed in the wrong time on purpose, to suggest that Rome's later hate for Carthage was somehow set into both cities' fates from the very start. It is a poet's point, not a historian's claim, and that gap matters.

Fearing the twins anyway as future rivals, Amulius ordered them drowned in the Tiber RiverTY-burthe river in central Italy on whose banks Rome was traditionally founded.. <!-- block 33 role=content voice imperative -->
Notice how closely this echoes an older story from Mesopotamia. An infant is set adrift on a river. He is saved against every chance. He is meant for far more than his own humble rescuers could have guessed. That is almost exactly the shape of Sargon's basket story. This does not prove that one story copied the other. It shows instead that this kind of story, a low, uncertain start hiding a great future, appealed to ancient people again and again, in far-apart cultures with no contact at all.

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
Grown to manhood, Romulus and Remus restored their grandfather Numitor to his throne. They then set out to found a city of their own, near where they had washed ashore. Rome itself rose in a part of Italy already shared with other peoples. Etruscan cities lay just to the north, and their customs shaped early Rome deeply. Greek trading colonies lay further south, in Sicily and along Italy's coast. The Greeks themselves called this whole southern cluster *Magna Graecia* MAG-nuh GREE-shuh"Greater Greece," the Greek trading colonies of southern Italy and Sicily., "Greater Greece." Livy's account of the twins does not end happily, though. The brothers argued bitterly over exactly where their new city's sacred boundary line, the **pomerium**, should run. Remus mocked his brother by leaping over the low wall Romulus had begun to mark out. In Livy's telling, Romulus killed his own twin brother on the spot for it. He said the same fate awaited anyone else who dared cross Rome's border uninvited. Tradition dates this founding, and this fratricideFRAT-rih-sydethe killing of one's own brother or sister., to 753 BC. Rome, in other words, begins its own story with a killing its own writers never tried to hide.

<!-- section: CITIES CARTHAGE AND ROME -->

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
**Carthage**, on the North African coast, and **Rome**, on the Tiber River in central Italy, were both founded as small, precarious settlements in the eighth century BC. Neither city's founders could have guessed the two would one day fight for control of the whole Mediterranean.
