<!-- section: A BARD'S MEMORY OF THE PALACE AGE -->

<!-- block 32 role=content -->
Tradition credits the poems known as the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* to a single poet named HomerHOH-murthe poet, or tradition of poets, credited with shaping the Iliad and Odyssey out of centuries of spoken storytelling.. Scholars remain split over whether one person composed them. Some think the name instead stands for a long line of oral storytellers, called bards. These bards kept shaping and reciting these same tales aloud, for many generations, before either poem was ever written down. Either way, both poems are a kind of epic poetryEP-ika long poem, often first passed down by speech, that tells the deeds of heroes and gods., long verse telling the deeds of great heroes and gods. Both poems also keep dim, half-true memories of the very Mycenaean world that had collapsed centuries earlier. Bronze weapons, boar-tusk helmets, and named kings and cities partly match what Linear B tablets actually recorded. All of this got filtered through three centuries of retelling. It got mixed with details, like iron weapons, that belong to the poet's own later age, not the Bronze Age his heroes supposedly lived in. Homer is not, in other words, a sure record of the Trojan War the way a court chronicle would be. He is something rarer, and in some ways worth more. He shows how later Greeks remembered a world they knew had once been greater than their own, and how they turned that memory into a story about right and wrong.

<!-- section: ACHILLES'S WRATH -->

<!-- block 37 role=content -->
The Iliad opens on a quarrel. Achillesuh-KIL-eezthe greatest warrior at Troy, whose wrath over a captive woman drives the whole plot of the Iliad., the greatest warrior fighting at Troy, and Agamemnonag-uh-MEM-noncommander of the Greek forces at Troy, whose quarrel with Achilles opens the Iliad., the leader of the whole Greek army, fall into a bitter fight. They argue over a captive woman named Briseis, whom Agamemnon claims for himself after being forced to give up a captive of his own. Furious at the insult to his honor, Achilles pulls out of the fighting entirely. The poem states plainly that this one choice, not any single battle, will cost the Greeks dearly. The *Iliad* 's opening word, in the old Greek, means "wrath." That is no accident. The whole poem is built around one question: what does Achilles's wounded pride cost everyone around him, friend and foe alike? This is a vice, not a virtue, and Homer does not hide it. He also never denies Achilles's real courage as a fighter. A hero can hold true excellence in one respect, what the Greeks called *arete* ah-reh-TAYexcellence, or the kind of honor-winning greatness a Greek hero strives for., and still cause real harm through a failure of self-control in another.

<!-- section: ODYSSEUS'S CUNNING, AND ITS OWN LIMITS -->

<!-- block 42 role=content -->
The Odyssey offers one of its most famous scenes. Odysseusoh-DIS-ee-usthe cunning hero of the Odyssey, famed for the tricks that get him and his crew out of danger. and his crew are trapped in the cave of the one-eyed Cyclops, Polyphemuspol-ih-FEE-musthe one-eyed Cyclops Odysseus blinds and tricks to escape his cave.. They escape only through Odysseus's quick thinking. He tricks the giant with a false name, "Nobody," then blinds his single eye before slipping away. Where Achilles's story studies harmful pride, Odysseus's story is, at least at first, a study in **prudence**: quick thinking, patience, and self-control used to survive a spot raw strength alone could never have escaped.

<!-- block 43 role=content -->
Yet Homer does not let Odysseus off entirely clean either. As his ship pulls away to safety, Odysseus cannot resist shouting his own real name back at the blinded giant. He boasts of his own wit, a boast that lets Polyphemus curse him in return. That curse haunts the rest of his voyage home. Even the clearest example of prudence holds a small lapse into the same kind of pride that undid Achilles, just on a smaller scale. In other words, no hero in these poems gets to be purely wise or purely foolish. Each one carries both traits at once. Homer's heroes, taken together, offer a full lesson in weighing virtue and vice honestly. Real courage, real cunning, and real pride, sometimes bought at a genuine cost, often live inside the very same person.

<!-- section: A NEW BEGINNING, LOOKING OUTWARD -->

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
Greece, by this point, has climbed a long way back from the Dark Age's low point. It is newly able to read and write, newly linked to Mediterranean trade, and it now has poems that let it remember, even if not perfectly, a lost heroic age. Around this same period, the Greek city, or *polis* POH-lisa Greek city-state, the basic unit of Greek government and community life., began taking shape as Greece's basic form of government. Each polis governed itself, with its own laws and its own citizens, rather than answering to one king over the whole land. In Athens, one polis among many, a lawgiver named SolonSOH-lunan Athenian lawgiver who reformed the city's laws in the early sixth century BC. would soon try to reform its laws. What Archaic Greece does not yet know is how close a far larger power has already come. The Ionian Greek cities along Anatolia's coast will soon fall under Persian rule.
