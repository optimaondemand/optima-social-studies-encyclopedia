<!-- block 2 role=content added -->
What Was Dramatic Performance In Athens Like? Dramatic performances were a major occasion in Athens. There was no larger gathering in the city than the audience at the Theater of Dionysus which held almost 20,000 spectators.

<!-- block 3 role=content added -->
What would it have been like to attend? What would you have seen and heard and how similar to or different would it have been from watching a play today?

<!-- block 4 role=content added -->
For the most part, the Greek theater would feel familiar, though there would be some things that were done differently. First, there is the theater itself. The Theater of Dionysus was laid out much like a contemporary theater: there is a stage in the front for the performers with seating facing it for the audience. There would be a backdrop behind the stage with art to create a setting and it was called skene meaning ‘tent’. You can probably already see that because its function was to set the scene, it is the origin of our word ‘scene’. The play would be performed by actors wearing costumes and there would be various props on the stage.

<!-- block 5 role=content added -->
You would notice some differences. First, the theater and stage itself. Most of our stages are elevated above the first several rows of seats. We can get away with this because of microphones, but the ancient Greeks had to rely entirely on acoustics and that meant putting the stage at the bottom of the theater with seating rising up around it. The geometry of the rising stone seating reflected sound upwards so that people in the farthest seats back could hear because they were in the highest seats. Whereas our theaters often just extend straight back with only enough rise to see over the heads of spectators in front, the Greek theaters used a steeper angle and a semi-circle shape around the stage to amplify the sound. The limestone materials absorbed lower frequency sounds like crowd noise but reflected high frequency speech.

<!-- block 6 role=content added -->
Given the rise of the seating, most of the audience are looking down upon the stage. The stage itself would include some props, but very few by modern standards. Greek theater tended to be more iconic and symbolic than realistic and hence the stage is relatively austere. Any props beyond some minimal scene-setting decorations would figure into the action in some important way. The principle of Chekhov’s gun applies: you never put a gun in a scene unless later a character shoots it. Famous examples that you’ll read about are the tapestry that Clytaemnestra rolls out for her husband Agamemnon to walk on in his bare feet and the boots that he unlaces and takes off to do so. These props drew attention to the significance of Agamemnon choosing to return home like a god or presumptuous Eastern king and emphasizes that his fate may not yet have been sealed: Clytaemnestra seems to be testing him.

<!-- block 7 role=content added -->
There were no lighting effects, but we know of creative sound effects made offstage using various objects to simulate sounds like thunder, rain, and battle. Interestingly, there was a rolling platform called ekkuklema that could simulate a chariot or boat moving. More famously, there was a mechanical crane that could lift actors into the air, simulating flight called the mechane. Often it was used to bring actors portraying the gods on stage as though they were coming 1 down from the heavens. This plot device–in the literal sense–was so common it gave rise to our phrase: deus ex machina or ‘god out of the machine’.

<!-- block 8 role=content added -->
There is also the shape to consider. Our stages today tend to be rectangular, but the stage here called an orchestra was circular and about twenty yards in diameter. The chorus would occupy the orchestra for the duration of the show and often dance in a ring formation around its edge. The circular shape helps with the semi-circle seating, but it also iterates the centrality of the chorus which is always front and center. The actors themselves were always entering and exiting stage.

<!-- block 9 role=content added -->
The reason for this is that each play, no matter how many speaking characters overall, had a cast of only three actors. That’s it. There could be non-speaking extras and sometimes particular members of the chorus might shout out a single line, but every other speaking part was performed by one of only three actors. (Oedipus Rex has ten characters.) Hence, each actor had to play multiple parts and the playwright could have at most three characters on stage at once. Sometimes a particularly intricate plot required actors switching parts mid-scene. A scene might involve several speaking characters rotating throughout. This would require an actor to quickly move off-scene and change costumes to return as a new character, but the complexity of combinations of characters cycling in and out might require that some character would be split between two or more actors or that actors end up swapping roles over the course of the scene. The actor playing Oedipus at the beginning of the scene might have to play Creon at the end, while the actor playing Antigone at the beginning plays Oedipus by the end.

<!-- block 10 role=content added -->
If you know the play Antigone, you know that she was the daughter of Oedipus, but she would have been portrayed by a male actor. How could a male actor convincingly play a woman and how could the sight of actors switching roles mid-scene fail to disorient the audience? The answer is that all the actors (and extras and chorus) wore masks. Again, these masks were iconic rather than realistic and so oversized with a single open-mouthed expression. Whether that expression was one of anger, fear, mourning, etc. communicated something about the character’s role in the plot. The open mouth contained a small funnel that functioned like a miniature megaphone, helping the actor’s voice to carry across the theater. The size of the mask alone was usually sufficient to conceal the identity of the actor. Additionally, actors typically wore a body stocking that could be padded either to give the appearance of large muscles in a heroic or divine male character or to provide a more feminine shape for a female character.

<!-- block 11 role=content added -->
Greek acting was difficult and required trained professionals. The mask itself made movement and speaking difficult and so actors had to have powerful and trained voices. Most of the acting we see today is on screen and the camera close-up has made our acting more realistic and subdued. Exaggerated facial expressions, movements, and voices look and sound bizarre when viewing a movie. But most of the audience here, in a Greek theater that holds 20,000 people, are at some distance from the actors. The over-sized mask is the only chance to give the audience any facial expression to see, and all gestures must be exaggerated to make a visual impression on the audience whereas the vocal parts must be overly-expressive and loud in 2 order to both convey emotion and be heard. An iconic language of gesture called schemata, used to convey emotion in any play, developed to become part of the actor’s skillset.

<!-- block 12 role=content added -->
The chorus made each Greek play partially what we’d call a musical with numerous song/dance performances. The costumes of the chorus tended to be more elaborate than the actors themselves as the chorus was the most visible human element on the stage. (The singing/dancing itself helped cover actor’s exits and entrances.) The chorus always functioned as some kind of character and often dramas were named after the character played by the chorus: Trojan Women and Libation Bearers are examples of tragedies named for the chorus. This practice is even more common in comedies; see Aristophanes Clouds, Frogs, Wasps, and Birds. The choral singing/dancing in comedies was much more lively and gave the performance a fun carnivalesque atmosphere. The costumes likewise resemble what we might see today at Mardis Gras in New Orleans or Carnival in Rio de Janeiro.

<!-- block 13 role=content added -->
At this point, you have to be wondering, who paid for all this? Professional actors, playwrights, choreographers, singing/dancing choruses, stagehands, musicians, and all of these extravagant costumes? Show business is big business today where producers, directors, and actors reap enormous payouts, but the production of Greek drama was very different.

<!-- block 14 role=content added -->
The production of Athenian drama for the weeklong City Dionysia was an enormous undertaking that involved both public and private expenditure. Whereas our film and television today is produced and funded as a business that makes a substantial profit, these Athenian productions were considered a public service or litourgi meaning ‘public work’. The overall organization and planning was performed by public officials, but the production was done at private expense by wealthy producers called choregoi from which our word ‘choreographer’ is derived. These producers made no profit from the festival; they funded these productions as a generous sacrifice for the common good of Athens. Such extravagant giving for the public is strange to us and we will need to explore the Greek concept of glory to understand it.

<!-- block 15 role=content added -->
Traditionally, glory was seen as the possession of outstanding individuals or heroes marked out from everyone else by their superior arete or excellence. This is the Heroic ethos we see so clearly in Homer and it was carried forth into the classical period by the wealthy elite of Athens and other cities. However, with the rise of the Polis, the idea of a collective glory of the city became prominent. Consider that the anonymous hoplite secures victory on the battlefield by working as part of a team, hence the glory belongs to the whole and not the individual. The ordinary commoner achieves glory only through the glory of the Polis. Hence, in the context of the polis, individual glory is an aristocratic value. Athens was not just any polis but a democracy that officially proclaimed all citizens to be essentially equal. Thus, there is significant tension between the aristocratic and democratic conceptions of glory in Athens. We will now see how the Athenians managed to accommodate and balance both visions of glory together in the City Dionysia.

<!-- block 16 role=content added -->
Athenians loved drama and took pride in their city’s excellence at it. During the approximately hundred years of the Athenian democracy, Athens dominated the Greek art of drama, defining the art, setting the standard, and producing most of the plays. We know that during this period, Athens produced roughly 2,000 dithyrambs, 1,250 tragedies, and 650 comedies. All this from a city that never had a population of free male citizens of more than 60,000 at its peak. Factoring in children, women, slaves and resident aliens, Athens’ population may have reached 250,000–about like Boise, Idaho today.

<!-- block 17 role=content added -->
Let’s get a feel for the scale of this undertaking. The City Dionysia was a significant event that demonstrated the wealth, power, and excellence of the city. We can call this manifestation or revelation of excellence glory. Held in March to mark the end of winter and beginning of spring, the themes were wine, intoxication, fertility, freedom, and the promise of new life.

<!-- block 18 role=content added -->
The first day saw an enormous festive procession escort the image or idol of Dionysus from the countryside of Attica and into the city proper. Priests and priestesses dressed in all their finery led the procession surrounded by many choruses singing and dancing to music. The crowd would have been loud and boisterous and the general mood was one of celebration. The best parallel today is the great Mardis Gras parade in New Orleans and the Brazilian version in Rio de Janeiro called Carnival. Throngs gathered to listen to the pipes and dance. Wine flowed everywhere–all paid for by the city. The celebration led up to a massive sacrifice of bulls–enough bulls for the entire city to feast. After the feasting, another more rowdy procession went through the city at night, led by torches.

<!-- block 19 role=content added -->
Athens devoted the second day to the Dithyramb competition. A dithyramb is a combination of hymn and dance performed by a chorus of 50. The people of Athens were divided into ten tribes and they competed against one another with each tribe putting forth 1 chorus of men and 1 chorus of boys. The dithyramb is a lively form of music accompanied by pipes and sometimes drums and the dancing was vigorous and fast-paced, requiring athleticism and practice. The choruses would have practiced their songs and dances for months, led and funded by a producer (choregoi) from the chorus’s tribe. In addition to choreography and leading the rehearsals, it was the job of the producer to pay for the elaborate costumes that the chorus wore. Colorful costumes, sometimes imitating animals and gods pleased the crowds and increased the chances of victory.

<!-- block 20 role=content added -->
The third through fifth days were all set aside for the tragic competition. Each day of this competition was devoted to the tragic trilogy of one poet (and a fourth unrelated comedic Satyr show.) Around June of the previous year, many poets had submitted proposals for these tragic plays and the highest public official in Athens, the Archon Eponymous, selected the three proposals he thought best. The poet received a stipend from the city to write the complete trilogy and then the city drafted a producer on his behalf. The city kept a register of citizens wealthy enough to produce a tragic trilogy and they selected choregoi by lottery. No wealthy citizen ever had to do it two years in a row and if the city overestimated a citizen’s wealth, he could appeal. This service was not optional but considered a public work litourgi and every bit as mandatory as we would think of jury duty today.

<!-- block 21 role=content added -->
Each tragic play had 3 professional actors, 15 members of the chorus, plus various musicians, extras, and stagehands too. The choregoi were responsible for finding rehearsal space, collaborating with the playwright–much like a modern producer–and leading the rehearsals of the chorus. The actors, chorus, and musicians were paid not only for their performance but for all their rehearsal time too. The choregoi also paid for all of the costumes, props, and scenic effects. The total cost of putting on a tragic production was more than 3,000 silver drachmas. In Athens, this amount of money would be equivalent to the yearly earnings of ten skilled workers.

<!-- block 22 role=content added -->
On the sixth day, the city enjoyed the comedy competition. Similar to the tragic competition, five playwrights had been selected by the Archon Eponymous to compete in the City Dionysia comedic competition. Like the tragedy competition, a wealthy citizen was enlisted under the liturgy system to sponsor the performance. The chorus of the comedy was larger with 24 members, but only one play had to be produced.

<!-- block 23 role=content added -->
All three competitions, the dithyrambic, tragic, and comedic were evaluated by the same ten judges. On the first day of the festival, each tribe selected four potential judges and one judge was chosen by lottery to represent each tribe. We should pause for a moment and observe that Athenian drama was, like almost everything the Greeks did, agonistic meaning competitive.

<!-- block 24 role=content added -->
Remember, that traditionally, Greek culture is incredibly competitive! Greeks loved contests and turned almost everything into a competition. Contests brought forth excellence and Greeks loved seeing excellence in every human endeavor whether athletics, sculpture, poetry, architecture, debate, drama, singing, or warfare. The logic of excellence was the same across all these practices: excellence is glorious and beautiful to behold and excellence requires overcoming some kind of resistance. Hence, competition brings out the most excellence.

<!-- block 25 role=content added -->
The Greek word for excellence is arete which is often translated as ‘virtue’. Today, ‘virtue’ conveys a narrowly moral meaning whereas for the ancient Greeks, arete meant excellence in any practice–from sailing to horseback riding to sprinting. What is arete? Arete is the capacity or ability to excel in the given activity. Thus, the arete of sprinting is speed. As the Greeks saw it, having excellence as a trait or ability is not enough; excellence in ability remains only potential until it is manifested in action. The revelation or display of excellence in deed is what the Greeks called glory. Achieving glory, whether on the battlefield, in the Olympic games, or at the City of Dionysia was thought to be among the greatest goods of life.

<!-- block 26 role=content added -->
We can understand the Greeks better if we keep in mind that glory was generally prized above wealth. Thus, it would have been hard for the Greeks to put on any performance admitting of degrees of excellence without making it a contest. If an activity can be performed better or worse based on the ability of the person, then it creates an opportunity for competitors to win glory. The practice of seeking glory in agonistic competition is called philotimon. This should help us see why the choregoi parted with their money willingly to sponsor these productions.

<!-- block 27 role=content added -->
The ancient Greeks recognized a special virtue for large-scale generosity aimed at the common good: the virtue of magnificence. Hence, while the choregoi made no financial profit, there was an opportunity here to display virtue and achieve glory. Prizes were awarded to the best actor in a tragedy and the best actor in a comedy. Then the overall best tragedy and comedy were selected. The playwrights who wrote and directed these winning plays received high honors and a monument which was placed outside the theater–permanently displaying their glory for all to see. The highest honors were reserved for the producers of these winning plays and the producers of the winning dithyrambs.

<!-- block 28 role=content added -->
And yet, the glory of a great performance would not solely belong to the outstanding individuals but ultimately accrued to Athens itself. A dramatic production was a cooperative endeavor requiring excellence from every member of the team–from the playwright and producer, the actors, and right on down to the anonymous members of the chorus and musicians and stagehands who all made it possible by contributing their own excellence to the activity of the group. The dithyramb competition was a major source of pride for the winning tribe as a whole whose chorus won and hence an avenue for civic glory.

<!-- block 29 role=content added -->
The city of Athens as a whole shared in the glory as the entire dramatic festival was the work of Athens, publicly organized and funded through the contribution of both public and private wealth. Only the professional actors might be from outside of Athens; producers and playwrights were wealthy citizens while the chorus was made up of ordinary Athenian citizens. This was a city-wide performance: every year, 28 choregoi recruited, employed, and trained approximately 1,200 citizens in these choruses from a city that never had more than 60,000 male citizens total. Tens of thousands of Greeks from other cities came to join in the festivities and witness the spectacle. Admission was charged mostly to control demand.

<!-- block 30 role=content added -->
While the agonistic nature of the competition highlighted the talent of outstanding individuals, the city made sure to place the competition in a context that emphasized the collective identity of the city and reinforced the common ties that bound citizens together. Prior to the performances on stage, the audience witnessed a parade of tribute from client states, a ceremony to publicly mark the manumission or freeing of slaves, the ten elected generals poured out libations to honor Dionysus, and there was a parade of war orphans. When a man died in battle, his children were raised and educated at state expense and presented with a suit of hoplite armor upon turning 18. Wearing their new armor, they paraded in front of the audience and were accorded special seats of honor in the theater (along with 67 priests and priestesses).

<!-- block 31 role=content added -->
Thus, the City Dionysia provided a framework that allowed individual excellence to be displayed in agonistic competition, but within a system that overall was cooperative, harmonious, and testified to greatness of the city as a whole.
