<!-- section: Why would Texans want a state of their own inside Mexico? -->

<!-- block 31 role=content -->
In 1824, Mexico adopted a **constitution** that gave real power to its states. That sounded wonderful to Texas **settlers**, who liked local control. But Texas was not made a state of its own. It was joined to a neighbor, and the trouble that followed lasted more than a decade.

<!-- section: Power for the states -->

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
After the **empire** fell, Mexico had to choose a new kind of government. In October 1824, a new **congress** finished a constitution that made Mexico a **republic**. It had a president, a congress, and courts. It also did something important. It divided power between the national government and the states.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
This idea is called federalism, and a country built on it is called **federal**. In a federal system, each state has its own government and makes many of its own laws, while the national government handles things like defense and money. The United States works this way. In Mexico, the constitution of 1824 made it the law of the land.

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
Mexico's lawmakers borrowed from two places. They looked at the Constitution of the United States, which **Arizpe** and others had studied closely. They also looked at the Spanish constitution of 1812, which had been written during the war against Napoleon and had given local governments a larger role. The result mixed ideas from both.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
The new constitution was not a copy of either model. It said that the Catholic Church would be the only religion of the nation. It created nineteen states and several territories. And it left a lot of room for the states to decide matters for themselves, which is exactly what many local leaders wanted.

<!-- section: Why this mattered to Texas -->

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
Texas settlers liked the idea of local government. Most of them had grown up in states of their own, with their own legislatures and courts. A federal republic seemed to promise that decisions about their daily lives would be made close to home. Many believed that Mexico now had a government much like the one they had left.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Think about the settlers' memories. Many had watched lawmakers in their home states argue in open halls and pass laws that touched their farms and their stores. They believed that a government works best when it is close enough for ordinary people to reach. A lawmaker who lives down the road is easier to talk to than a minister in a far city.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
That hope shaped how they saw their future. Austin and others spoke warmly about the constitution of 1824. They took an **oath** to defend it. Later, when the constitution was in danger, they would fight to protect it, because it was the legal foundation of their right to govern their own affairs.

<!-- section: One state, two peoples -->

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
But Texas did not get a state of its own. The constitution did not say how many people a state needed, and Texas had too few. In 1824, a state law joined Texas to its neighbor to the southwest. The new state was called **Coahuila y Tejas**. The name was long, and so were the miles.

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
Coahuila had far more people than Texas. In the state legislature, Texans were always outvoted. The capital was at **Saltillo**, hundreds of miles from the **colonies** on the **Brazos**, across hard country. A lawmaker from Texas might spend weeks on the road just to take his seat.

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
The state courts were far away too. A settler with a land dispute might need to send for papers or travel for weeks to get an answer. Laws were written in Spanish by people who had never seen a Texas farm. Texans began to feel that decisions about them were being made by people who did not know them.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
Think about the different interests. Coahuila was mostly dry ranching and mining country. Texas was filling with cotton farmers and growing towns. The two parts of the state wanted different roads, different land laws, and different kinds of help. Even honest lawmakers in Saltillo had trouble serving both.

<!-- section: The first petitions -->

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
Almost at once, Texans began asking for a state of their own. A request for statehood within Mexico was not **rebellion**. It was a way of asking Mexico to carry out its own constitution. Texans made the request politely, and they made it again and again.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
The Mexican government always found a reason to wait. Texas was too small, officials said, or the time was wrong, or the country had other worries. Each delay made the petitioners more impatient. Over the next decade the request never stopped, and each refusal made it harder to believe that the system could be changed from within.

<!-- section: Strong on paper -->

<!-- block 73 role=content -->
The constitution of 1824 gave Mexico a good framework, but it depended on habits that the country had not yet built. Federal systems work when people trust the rules, when states obey the national government, and when leaders accept the results of elections. These habits grow slowly, and Mexico had been free for only three years.

<!-- block 74 role=content voice imperative -->
Compare Mexico's problem with the problem of the United States. The American states had been colonies with their own assemblies for more than a century before they joined together. Mexico's **provinces** had been ruled from the capital by Spain's viceroys for three hundred years. The states had little practice at governing themselves, and the capital had little practice at sharing power.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
Several things were missing. The **treasury** was weak, the army was powerful, and many leaders cared more about their own **region** than the whole country. A constitution is only as strong as the people who are willing to follow it. Its weaknesses were already visible in the first years.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
Texas stood in an unusual position. The settlers were among the strongest supporters of the federal system, because they liked its promise of local control. If that system failed, they stood to lose more than most. This is one reason that the story of Texas and the story of Mexico are so closely tied.

<!-- block 77 role=content -->
What the settlers wanted was simple: a legislature near home, courts they could reach, and laws that fit their lives. The constitution of 1824 seemed to offer these things. For a decade, many Texans believed that their hopes could be met inside Mexico. Their belief rested on a document, and a document needs people willing to defend it.
