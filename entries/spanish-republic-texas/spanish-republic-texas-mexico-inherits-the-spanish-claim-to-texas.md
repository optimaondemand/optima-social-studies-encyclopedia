<!-- section: Empty land and empty pockets -->

<!-- block 45 role=content -->
In 1821, Mexico's government looked at its northern **frontier** and saw trouble. Texas had only a few thousand settlers. The war had wrecked towns and ranches, and the government had very little money to rebuild them. The **Comanche** **raided** deep into northern Mexico, far beyond Texas, and Mexican soldiers could not stop them.

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
The numbers tell the story. In 1821, a few thousand people lived in a province that was larger than France. Most lived in San Antonio and a few other places, and the rest of the land was held by Native peoples or by no one. A government that could not protect its own towns could not protect a country-sized frontier.

<!-- block 49 role=content -->
There was also a danger on the other side. The United States had bought Louisiana in 1803, and its settlers were moving west. American adventurers had already crossed into Texas more than once. Mexican leaders worried that if they did nothing, Americans would simply take the land.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
The new government had its own troubles. It had to build a nation out of a war, and its leaders did not even agree on what kind of government to have. They worried about money, about the army, and about **rivals** at home. A far northern province with a few thousand people was not at the top of the list. But it was not a problem that could be ignored for long.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
The United States and Spain had argued over Texas for years. In 1819, a **treaty** set the border between them at the Sabine River on the east, but some Americans still said that Texas belonged to the United States. Mexican leaders knew about this. They knew that a border on a map did not stop people who wanted land.

<!-- section: People who will defend their own land -->

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
The solution was an old one with a new twist. If the province needed people, perhaps it could invite them. Settlers who owned land in Texas would have a reason to defend it against raiders. They would also make a buffer against the dangers of both the Comanche and other foreigners.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
Spain had used the same idea when it brought the Canary Islanders to San Antonio in 1731. The new twist was who the settlers would be. This time they would come from another country, and they would come in much larger numbers.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
It was a risky plan. The new settlers might not be loyal. But the officials who made it believed the risk of doing nothing was larger. They also believed that settlers from the United States, who knew how to farm and fight on a frontier, were the best choice. The laws required them to follow Mexican law and to become Catholics.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
Officials also hoped that settlers would trade with Mexico, pay taxes, and make the land produce. A frontier with farms and ranches would feed soldiers, supply towns, and bring in money. A frontier with nothing would only cost money. In their minds, a risky plan was better than a sure failure.

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
Not every Mexican leader liked the plan. Some feared that people from the United States would never become loyal Mexicans. They pointed to the language, the religion, and the habits that the settlers would bring. Supporters answered that the settlers would be bound by **oath** and by law. The argument went on for years, and it did not go away.

<!-- section: A request in San Antonio -->

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
The invitation began even before **independence**. Moses Austin was a Connecticut-born businessman who had moved to Spanish Missouri to mine lead. A financial panic in 1819 ruined him. He decided to make a new start in Texas. In December 1820, he traveled to San Antonio with a **petition** to settle three hundred families.

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
Austin's reasons were plain. He needed money, and he thought Texas could give it to him. He had already seen how a settlement could grow on the frontier in Missouri. He believed that good land and a few hundred families could do the same in Texas. It was a business plan, but it was also a plan to rebuild his life.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
The governor at first told him to leave. But Austin met an old acquaintance, the Baron de **Bastrop**, who spoke for him. In January 1821, the Spanish authorities approved his request. The plan was now official. The man who had started it would not live to carry it out.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
Bastrop mattered. He knew the officials and he spoke for Austin. His help turned a refusal into a yes. A different governor, or a different friend, might have meant a different story. Small things like this can change everything that comes after.

<!-- section: The rules for settlers -->

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
After independence, Mexico wrote its own laws. The first was the Imperial Colonization Law of January 1823. It offered land to families who would settle. Next came the General Colonization Law of August 1824, which left the details to the Mexican states. Texas was joined with its neighbor, and the state of **Coahuila y Tejas** passed its own law in 1825.

<!-- block 72 role=content voice imperative -->
Look at how the laws changed. The 1823 law came from an **emperor**, **Iturbide**, who soon fell from power. The 1824 law came from the new **republic**. The 1825 law came from the state government. Each time, the government changed, but the idea stayed the same. That shows how much Mexico's leaders wanted settlers, even when they could not agree on much else.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
The 1825 law set up the **colony agent** (*empresario*) system. A government contract allowed an empresario to bring in families, usually a few hundred, within a set number of years. In return, the empresario received land. Farmers could receive a plot of about 177 acres. Ranchers could receive a **league**, which is about 4,428 acres. Settlers had to swear loyalty to Mexico and accept the Catholic faith.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
The terms were generous. Land was nearly free, and Mexico saw this as the price of a safe frontier. What Mexico did not yet know was how many people would accept the offer, and what they would bring with them.

<!-- block 79 role=content -->
There were conditions. A settler had to farm the land, build a home, and take an oath. The **colony** agent had to bring in the number of families promised, or the contract could be canceled. Some limits were put on settling near the border or the coast. These rules showed that Mexico wanted settlers, but it also wanted to stay in control.
