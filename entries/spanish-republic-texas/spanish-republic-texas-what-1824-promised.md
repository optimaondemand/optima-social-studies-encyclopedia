<!-- section: GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY -->

<!-- block 14 role=content -->
**federalism** (FED-er-uh-liz-um) — a system of government in which power is divided between a national government and individual states, with states holding real authority of their own.

<!-- section: PART 1 ONE EMPRESARIO BREAKS THE PATTERN -->

<!-- block 16 role=content -->
Keeping the Mexican form and working inside it was not the only response available. In 1826, empresario Haden Edwards had his own grant revoked by Mexican authorities over a land dispute. Edwards and a small band of followers responded by declaring their own short-lived "Republic of Fredonia" in East Texas. It collapsed within months, and notably, Austin's own colonial militia helped Mexican forces put it down. Austin's colonists, still working the system rather than breaking from it, saw Fredonia as a reckless exception, not a model to follow.

<!-- section: WATCH OUT -->

<!-- block 18 role=content voice imperative -->
Hold onto the irony here. Austin helps suppress a rebellion against Mexican authority in 1827. Six years later, he will be arrested for disloyalty to that same authority. Watch how his relationship with Mexico City actually changes between these two moments.

<!-- block 19 role=content -->
The bigger political story that same decade was the federal **federalism** written into Mexico's Constitution of 1824, drawn partly from the United States' own constitutional model and partly from Spain's earlier Constitution of 1812. On paper, this gave Texas a real claim to self-governing power as part of a Mexican state.

<!-- section: PART 2 OUTVOTED FROM THE START -->

<!-- block 21 role=content -->
Texas was not given its own statehood. It was attached to its larger neighbor as the single state of "Coahuila y Tejas," with its capital at Saltillo, hundreds of miles from most Texas settlements. Texas colonists were consistently outvoted by Coahuila's larger population, and the province's own courts sat a long ride away. Petitions asking for separate statehood begin almost immediately after the merger, and they never really stop for the rest of the decade.

<!-- block 22 role=content -->
One more federal decision from this period touched the colony directly. In 1829, Mexico's government issued a decree abolishing slavery nationwide. In practice, Texas received a special exemption, and colonists who still wanted enslaved labor increasingly relied on a legal workaround: binding enslaved people under long-term "indentured servant" contracts that Mexican authorities mostly tolerated rather than enforced against.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW -->

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
This is the same pattern showing up again: a Mexican law stays on the books, and colonists build a practical workaround that keeps the law's form while changing what actually happens underneath it.
