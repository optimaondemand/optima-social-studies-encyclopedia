<!-- section: Three buildings were supposed to make a colony. What went wrong? -->

<!-- block 29 role=content tool_mention -->
Spain had a plan for the northern **frontier**. It was a set of three institutions, each with its own job. A priest, a soldier, and a **settler** would work together to turn a wild frontier into a Spanish **province**. This lesson looks at how that plan was supposed to work, and how it worked in Texas.

<!-- section: Three institutions, one goal -->

<!-- block 42 role=content -->
Spain built its frontier with three tools. The first was the mission. Priests would **congregate** Native people into a community, teach them the Christian faith, and train them in farming and trades. The second was the fort (*presidio*). Soldiers lived there and protected both the mission and the road. The third was the town (*pueblo*). This was a civilian town where Spanish settlers would live and eventually take over.

<!-- block 46 role=content -->
The plan had an end date. After about ten years, the mission was supposed to hand its land and its people over to the Church and the local government. The Native people would be Spanish subjects. The priests would move on to a new frontier and start again.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
This was a real idea with a real logic. Spain had seen it work before, and it did not need a large army to carry it out. A few priests, a small group of soldiers, and a handful of families could, on paper, turn a whole **region** into a Spanish province. That is why the plan appealed to officials who were always short of money. The question was whether the places Spain tried it on looked anything like the places where it had worked before.

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
The design came from earlier success in Mexico, where missions had grown up among peoples who already farmed. It was also cheap. A mission cost far less than an army. By turning Native people into farmers and taxpayers, the plan promised that a frontier could pay for itself.

<!-- section: Work, worship, and water -->

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
At San Antonio, five missions eventually stood along the river. Each had a church, workshops, and fields. The mission day followed the church bells. People prayed, worked, ate, and slept to a schedule. They learned to weave, build, farm, and care for cattle. Many were taught Spanish.

<!-- block 52 role=content -->
A visitor to a mission would have seen a walled compound. Inside were a church, a storehouse for grain, living rooms, and workshops. Outside were orchards, fields, and pastures. Cattle and sheep grazed on the land around it. A mission could grow much of its own food, and extra corn and beef went to the presidio.

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
The mission also changed who people were. A person who joined was given a Christian name and a place in the mission's work schedule. Children were baptized and taught prayers. The priests believed they were doing good. Many Native families, on the other hand, were giving up their old ways of finding food and their old ties to relatives. It was a big trade, and not everyone who made it was glad.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Water was the key. Missions dug **acequias** from the river to carry water to the fields. Some of those ditches still run in San Antonio today. They are one of the lasting marks of Spanish settlement. This way of bringing water to crops is called **irrigation**.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
But life at a mission was also hard. People who had lived freely were now expected to follow a strict routine. And disease was a constant danger. **Epidemics** of **smallpox** and measles swept through the crowded missions. Epidemics killed so many people that missions needed new arrivals just to stay the same size. Many Native people came to missions voluntarily, for food and for protection from their enemies, especially the **Apache** and later the **Comanche**.

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
People did not always stay. Some left to return to their old way of life, especially when food was short or when sickness struck. Priests sometimes sent soldiers to bring them back. That shows an important truth about the system. A mission worked only as long as people had a reason to stay, or no better place to go.

<!-- section: Some peoples joined, others did not -->

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
The system worked best with small groups who had lost land and food to enemies. In South Texas, many groups often called **Coahuiltecan** joined the missions. They were hunter-gatherers living in a harsh, dry land, and the missions offered them a more certain meal.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
The system worked less well with the **Caddo** of East Texas. They already had farms and towns and leaders. They did not need the missions, and they did not want to give up their own way of life. Many of them preferred to trade with the French. The East Texas missions struggled and, in time, closed.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
And with the Comanche, the system failed completely. A mounted people who followed the **bison** could not be gathered into a mission. They had no reason to join, and a great deal of power to resist. Why the Comanche were so powerful is a story of its own.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
Two more facts are worth noticing. Missions kept Native people near Spanish settlements, which made them a kind of shield against **raids**. And missions sometimes sheltered people who were fleeing war or enemies. A mission was never only a prison or only a refuge. It was a mixture, and different people experienced it differently.

<!-- section: Families from the Canary Islands -->

<!-- block 73 role=content -->
Spain had a hard time getting civilians to move to Texas. Almost nobody wanted to go. In 1731, the **Crown** paid for about fifteen families from the Canary Islands, off the coast of Africa, to cross the ocean and settle at San Antonio. They founded a town beside the presidio and mission. It was the only large group of civilian settlers Spain ever sent to Texas at its own expense.

<!-- block 74 role=content -->
Think about how those families must have felt. They left their homes on an island with green hills and rain, crossed the ocean, and then traveled across Mexico to a dry frontier far from anything they knew. They were promised land, special rights, and the chance to choose their own town leaders. They were also promised protection from raiders. The promises were easier to make than to keep.

<!-- block 75 role=content voice -->
That tells you how thin the Spanish effort really was. A system built on missions, soldiers, and a few dozen families could hold a few places. It could not fill a province. For that, Spain would have needed what it had in central Mexico: millions of people already living and working on the land.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
A civilian town needed settlers who would stay. But few people wanted to move to a place with so little protection and so few rewards. Most of the people in Texas towns were soldiers' families, retired soldiers, and Native people who had left the missions. The plan for the town had always depended on a stream of settlers that never came.
