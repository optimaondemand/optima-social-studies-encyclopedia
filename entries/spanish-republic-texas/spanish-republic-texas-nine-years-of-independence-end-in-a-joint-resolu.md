<!-- section: Nine years of independence end in a joint resolution. -->

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
President Tyler's annexation treaty failed in the Senate in June 1844. The election that followed settled the matter: a joint resolution of Congress passed in March 1845, and Texas formally accepted the terms that December.

<!-- section: PART 1 THE TERMS OF UNION -->

<!-- block 13 role=content -->
Tyler's 1844 treaty failed to win the two-thirds Senate vote a treaty required. The dispute was significant enough that it had to be settled through the presidential election that followed, after which Congress passed a joint resolution instead, requiring only a simple majority. That resolution passed in March 1845, and Texas formally accepted its terms that December. Texas kept its public lands and its existing public debt, and reserved the right to divide into as many as five states in the future, a concession specific to Texas's unique situation as a former independent republic.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW -->

<!-- block 15 role=content -->
No other new state joined the Union this way. Keeping its own public lands, keeping its own debt, and reserving a right to divide into five states are all terms unique to Texas's situation as a former independent republic, not the standard path any territory takes to statehood.

<!-- section: PART 2 ONE QUESTION LEFT UNANSWERED -->

<!-- block 17 role=content tool_mention -->
The joint resolution left one major question undefined on purpose: where, exactly, does the border between Texas and Mexico actually run? Try the interactive below to see what is actually at stake in that question.

<!-- block 20 role=content -->
This unresolved boundary carried straight into the years that followed. A nation that could neither fund itself nor defend its own frontier saw union with the United States as the answer to both problems, even with this question left open.
