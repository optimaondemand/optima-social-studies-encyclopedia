<!-- section: The inspection of 1828 -->

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
In 1828, the Mexican government sent General **Mier** y Terán to inspect the border with the United States. He was a careful man, trained as a scientist and an engineer. He traveled through Texas for months, counting people, measuring land, and talking with settlers and with Native peoples. He wrote down what he saw.

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
What he saw alarmed him. American settlers far outnumbered Mexicans in East Texas. They spoke English, followed American customs, and kept in close touch with the United States. Many of them knew little about Mexican law and cared less. Mier y Terán feared that Mexico might lose Texas without a shot being fired.

<!-- block 49 role=content -->
His own feelings were mixed. Mier y Terán admired the energy of the settlers and praised the richness of the land. He saw clearly that Texas could become wealthy, and that was part of the danger. A rich land full of people who looked north toward the United States was a prize that another country might someday want.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
His report suggested several remedies. Mexico should put soldiers and forts in key places. It should bring in Mexican and European families to settle the land. It should encourage trade between Texas and the rest of Mexico instead of with the United States. In short, Mexico should act before the situation grew worse.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
Think about how this looked from the capital. To officials in Mexico City, the settlers were guests who had been invited for a purpose. Now a respected general said that the guests outnumbered their hosts and might not leave. The report did not call for war, but it left leaders deeply uneasy.

<!-- section: The Law of April 6, 1830 -->

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
In April 1830, President **Bustamante** 's government passed a law based on those fears. It said that no more settlers from the United States could come into Texas. It called for new forts and soldiers along the coast and the border. And it said that Mexico would collect its customs, which are taxes on goods brought into the country.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
The law did not push out the settlers who already lived in Texas, and Austin worked to make sure that his **colony** 's contracts stayed in force. But the message was clear. Mexico City no longer trusted the colonists. Families who had planned to join relatives in Texas now found the road closed.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
Settlers resented the law for several reasons. They felt that it treated them as suspects instead of neighbors. The soldiers sent to enforce it were often convicts or poorly paid men who caused trouble. And the **tariffs** hurt farmers who depended on imported tools and supplies. A government that seemed to be pulling away was creating the very distance it feared.

<!-- section: A fort on the bay -->

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
One of the new forts stood at **Anahuac**, a small port on Galveston Bay. It had a customs house, and the officer in charge was Colonel Bradburn. Bradburn was strict about the rules. Settlers who shipped goods through the port had to pay taxes and follow **regulations** that they thought were unfair.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
Think about what a customs house meant for a settler. In earlier years, ships had come and gone with very little interference. Now each cargo had to be counted, taxed, and approved by an officer who spoke another language. Farmers who had learned to expect little from their government suddenly felt it at every dock.

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
Tempers rose in 1832. Bradburn arrested a young lawyer named William B. Travis and another settler, and he refused to let them go. Neighbors gathered and marched on the fort. For some weeks the two sides faced each other, and there was a real danger of a fight.

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
The settlers wrote a statement, called the Turtle **Bayou** **Resolutions**, to explain what they were doing. They did not say that they were rebelling against Mexico. They said that they supported General **Santa Anna**, who had risen against President Bustamante and claimed to be fighting for the **federal** **constitution** of 1824. They presented themselves as defenders of the real Mexico.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
This was a clever and sincere move. A rebel against the government is a rebel. But a defender of the constitution has the law on his side. The settlers wanted to show that their quarrel was with one officer and one unfair law, and not with Mexico. Soon, Mexican army officers in Texas gave in, and Bradburn left.

<!-- section: Two meetings at San Felipe -->

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
After Anahuac, Texans decided to speak with one voice. In October 1832, delegates from across the colonies met at **San Felipe** for a convention, and Austin helped lead it. Six months later, in April 1833, they met again. Delegates wrote **petitions** to the Mexican government and chose men to carry them south.

<!-- block 74 role=content -->
It helps to read what they actually asked. They did not ask for **independence**. They asked for **statehood** inside Mexico, so that Texas would no longer be tied to **Coahuila**. They asked Mexico to **repeal** the Law of April 6, so that families could come again. They asked for relief from the tariffs. And they asked for trial by **jury**.

<!-- block 77 role=content -->
Each of these requests was a request to be treated as a full and equal part of Mexico. Texans did not claim anything that the constitution of 1824 did not seem to offer. Notice how reasonable the list is. A people planning to break away would not likely spend its time asking for mail service and court **reform**.

<!-- block 78 role=content -->
But Mexico City was not an easy audience. The government had many other problems, and it saw the Texans through the eyes of General Mier y Terán's report. The more Texans argued that they were loyal, the more some officials suspected that they were bargaining. Each side believed that the other was not fully honest, and trust was running out.

<!-- section: A gap that kept widening -->

<!-- block 80 role=content -->
By 1833, two stories were being told. In one, Texas was a loyal and patient part of Mexico that asked only for what the constitution promised. In the other, Texas was a growing foreign colony that would someday demand to leave. Both stories contained some truth, and both led to action.

<!-- block 81 role=content -->
Think about how each side read the same events. A Mexican officer saw armed crowds marching on a fort. A settler saw citizens defending a neighbor from an unlawful arrest. Neither view was foolish. The sadness of these years is that each side had good reasons to be afraid.

<!-- block 82 role=content -->
Austin, as always, chose patience. He packed the petitions and set out for Mexico City, determined to explain what Texans wanted and to remove the doubts. His journey would take him to the center of Mexican power, and it would not end the way that he hoped.
