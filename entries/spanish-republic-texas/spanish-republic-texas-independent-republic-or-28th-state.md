<!-- section: Could Texas survive on its own? -->

<!-- block 29 role=content tool_mention -->
By the early 1840s, Texas had been independent for five years. It had won its war, but it still had no peace with Mexico, no money, and no way to defend its border. This lesson follows a string of bold and costly adventures, and the foreign powers who began to take an interest in the **Republic**.

<!-- section: The Santa Fe Expedition -->

<!-- block 42 role=content -->
President Lamar had big plans. In 1841, he sent about three hundred men, with wagons of goods, on a long journey across the plains to Santa Fe, in New Mexico. Texas claimed that its western border reached the **Rio Grande**, and Lamar hoped that the people of Santa Fe would welcome Texan rule and trade.

<!-- block 46 role=content -->
It was a bad plan from the start. The party was not sure of the way. They wandered for weeks across dry plains, ran short of food and water, and met hostile bands of Native warriors. By the time they reached New Mexico, they were worn out and spread thin.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
The New Mexican governor, **Armijo**, had been warned. His soldiers captured the Texans without a fight. The prisoners were marched hundreds of miles south to Mexico City, and many were held in prison for months. Lamar's dream of a Texas that reached to Santa Fe had cost the Republic many of its best men.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
Think about what this showed. Texas was a young and small country, and it had tried something that only a great power could do. A failure like this made the Republic look weak to its neighbors. It also showed that Texas leaders did not always plan carefully before they acted.

<!-- section: Raids on San Antonio -->

<!-- block 52 role=content -->
Mexico never accepted the loss of Texas, and in 1842 it showed that it was still a threat. In March, a small Mexican force under General **Vásquez** rode into San Antonio. The town was nearly empty, because most people had fled, and the Mexican troops soon left again. But the point was made.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
Think about how a **raid** felt to a family in San Antonio. Word came that soldiers were on the road, and people left their homes with whatever they could carry. A small force with no plan to stay could still empty a town. The Republic had no way to guard every road, and everyone knew it.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
In September, a larger force under General **Woll** captured San Antonio again and held it for a time. A group of Texans rode to help, and Woll's troops killed many of them in a fight known as the Dawson **Massacre**. Woll **retreated** south, taking prisoners with him.

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
Texans were furious. The new president, Sam Houston, was in his second term, and many of his people demanded a revenge. But Houston knew that the Republic had little money and no real army. He moved cautiously, which made him unpopular again. Many men decided to go to war without him.

<!-- section: Across the Rio Grande -->

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
In the late fall of 1842, a force of about three hundred Texan **volunteers** marched to the Rio Grande. They meant to punish Mexico for the raids. Their orders were to protect the border, but many of the men had other ideas. They crossed the river anyway, and attacked the Mexican town of Mier on December 26.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
The Texans fought well in the streets and killed many Mexican soldiers. But the Mexican force was much larger. After a day of fighting, the Texan leaders decided to give up, thinking that they would be treated as prisoners of war. They were marched south into Mexico.

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
Along the way, the prisoners made a daring break for freedom. They overpowered their guards and escaped into the mountains. But the country was dry and hard, and they had no food. Within days, most were recaptured, starving and weak. **Santa Anna**, who had returned to power in Mexico, was furious.

<!-- section: The black bean lottery -->

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
Santa Anna ordered that the recaptured men be punished. He wanted every tenth man shot. To decide who would die, the guards used a lottery. They put one bean in a jar for each prisoner. Seventeen of the beans were black, and the rest were white. Each prisoner, one by one, had to reach in and draw a bean.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
The men who drew white beans lived. The men who drew black beans were shot. A prisoner named William Preston Stapp later wrote about it, and his story is one of the most chilling accounts of the war. He described how calmly many of the men took their beans, and how they met their end.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
The black bean lottery shocked Texas. It stirred up new anger against Mexico. At the same time, it showed how costly it was to go to war without a plan. Seventeen men died because of a risky campaign, and the surviving prisoners spent months in Mexican prisons.

<!-- section: Britain and France -->

<!-- block 73 role=content -->
While Texas struggled, powerful countries across the ocean began to pay attention. Britain and France both recognized Texas as an independent nation. They saw several benefits. Texas could supply cotton for their mills, and it could be a buyer for their goods. It could also be a barrier to the growth of the United States.

<!-- block 74 role=content -->
Think about Britain's interest from a map. Texas had a long coast on the Gulf of Mexico, wide plains for cotton, and a growing number of **settlers**. A friendly Texas could give British ships a base in a **region** that the United States considered its own backyard. Distant powers had good reasons to care.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
Britain had another goal as well. It had ended slavery in its own **empire**, and many British leaders hoped to see it ended elsewhere. Some of them thought that an independent Texas, tied to Britain by trade, might one day be persuaded to move away from slavery. Texans listened politely, and some hoped for British help against Mexico.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
Houston understood the game. He let it be known that Texas was talking with Britain and France. He did not want to leave the Republic in the hands of a foreign power. But he hoped that the news would make leaders in Washington worry, and make them more willing to offer annexation. It was a clever use of a weak hand.

<!-- section: Why Washington worried -->

<!-- block 78 role=content -->
The British interest alarmed Americans a great deal. A cotton-growing, slaveholding republic under British influence, right on the border of the United States, seemed dangerous. Some feared that Britain would build ports and forts there. Others feared that it would use its influence to end slavery in Texas.

<!-- block 79 role=content -->
This alarm was greater in Washington than in Mexico City. Mexico wanted Texas back and was not afraid of British help. But American leaders worried about their own border, their own trade, and their own future. They began to think that **annexing** Texas might be safer than leaving it alone.

<!-- block 80 role=content -->
By 1844, Texas sat in a very odd position. It was independent, but it could not pay its **debts**. It had friends and enemies on every side. The choice between staying independent and joining the United States was becoming the central question in the Republic, and the United States was starting to ask it too.
