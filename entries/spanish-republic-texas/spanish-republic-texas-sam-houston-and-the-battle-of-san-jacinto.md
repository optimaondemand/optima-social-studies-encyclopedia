<!-- section: Can a general win a war by running away? -->

<!-- block 28 role=content tool_mention -->
After the **Alamo** and **Goliad**, the **Texian** cause seemed lost. **Santa Anna** had a large army and Texas had a small one. For six weeks, General Sam Houston **retreated** east, while thousands of **settlers** fled before him. Then, in a single afternoon, everything changed. This lesson follows that long retreat and the eighteen minutes that ended the war.

<!-- section: The Runaway Scrape -->

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
When news of the Alamo reached the Texian settlements, panic spread. Families loaded wagons, gathered children, and set out east toward the Louisiana border. Rain had turned the roads to mud, rivers were swollen, and many people walked. The flight was called the Runaway Scrape, and thousands took part.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
The Texian army was only a few hundred men when Houston took command in March. He pulled back from **Gonzales** and began to march east, with the Mexican army behind him. The government of the new **republic** also fled, first to Harrisburg and then to Galveston Island. Texas was losing its homes and its capital at once.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
Think about what this looked like to the people who were running. They had heard that the Alamo had fallen and that Goliad had been a slaughter. They had no army that seemed able to protect them. Many of them cursed Houston for retreating when he should have been fighting.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
Houston heard the complaints. Government officials urged him to stop and fight, and his own soldiers grew restless. Some officers talked of choosing a new general. Houston held on to his command and kept moving, because he believed that one battle lost would end Texas for good.

<!-- section: Houston's reasons -->

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
Houston had a plan, or at least he said so later. Texas had one army. If he risked it in an open fight against a larger force, as Fannin had done, and lost, there would be nothing left. He wanted to gather strength, train his men, and wait until Santa Anna made a mistake.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
He also knew that Santa Anna had divided his army. The Mexican general had sent columns in several directions to chase settlers and the Texian government. Each column was weaker than the whole, and each moved farther from supplies. Time, Houston believed, was working against the army that was far from home.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
At Groce's plantation on the **Brazos**, Houston stopped for almost two weeks. His men drilled, rested, and received two small **cannon**, a gift from the people of Cincinnati, Ohio, called the Twin Sisters. Reinforcements arrived. When he finally marched, his army numbered around nine hundred.

<!-- section: Santa Anna overreaches -->

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
Think about what the time at Groce's plantation did. Many of Houston's men had never drilled together. They learned to march, to load in order, and to hold their fire until they were told. That discipline would matter when they crossed the meadow at San Jacinto, because a ragged charge would have broken under the Mexican guns.

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
In mid-April, Santa Anna pushed ahead with a small, fast force, hoping to catch the Texian government and end the war at once. He burned the town of Harrisburg, found the government gone, and then learned that Houston's army was close by. He had outrun his supplies and most of his own army.

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
Houston turned toward him. On April 19, the Texians reached a spot where Buffalo **Bayou** meets the San Jacinto River. Santa Anna made camp on a low piece of ground with **marsh** behind him and the bayou on one side. It was a poor place to fight, though he did not seem to see it.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
Reinforcements reached Santa Anna on the morning of April 21, which raised his force to about 1,300 men. Houston had a few hundred fewer. But that morning, a **scout** destroyed the only bridge across the bayou behind Santa Anna's camp. Now neither army could easily escape, and Houston knew where the fight would take place.

<!-- section: Eighteen minutes -->

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
The Mexican soldiers were resting in the afternoon sun when the Texians attacked at about half past four. They came across a long meadow, holding their fire until they were close. Then they poured volleys into the camp, and ran forward shouting, "Remember the Alamo! Remember Goliad!"

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
The Mexican army was caught off guard. Many soldiers had no time to form a line. Officers shouted orders that nobody heard. The soldiers who tried to run found the marsh behind them and the bayou beside them. There was no room for the **cavalry** to maneuver and no way for anyone to retreat.

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
Anger drove many of the Texian soldiers. Some had friends or relatives who had died at the Alamo and Goliad. They fought with a fury that was hard to control, and some of them kept on killing after the battle was won. Houston and other officers tried to stop it, with mixed success.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
The main fight lasted about eighteen minutes. After that, the Texians pursued the survivors across the plain. Hundreds of Mexican soldiers were killed, and hundreds more were taken prisoner. The Texians lost fewer than a dozen men. It was one of the most lopsided victories in American history.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
The next day, soldiers found a man hiding in the grass, dressed in a plain private's coat. When the prisoners saw him, some called him "El Presidente." It was Santa Anna. The president of Mexico was now a **captive**, and the war was almost over.

<!-- section: Why this ground? -->

<!-- block 72 role=content voice imperative -->
Look at what the ground at San Jacinto did. Mexican cavalry, so dangerous at **Coleto** Creek, could not use its speed in a marsh. Mexican soldiers could not form the lines that made their **muskets** deadly. And no one could run away, because the bridge was gone. The place took away every advantage the Mexican army had.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
Houston did not simply rely on luck. He picked the moment and the place, he held his men back until they were close, and he used the surprise of a late-day attack. But luck was also on his side. If Santa Anna had camped on higher ground, or if his other columns had been closer, the result could have been very different.

<!-- section: Houston and his critics -->

<!-- block 77 role=content -->
Historians still weigh Houston's own account of the campaign. In his official report, he describes a careful plan. He retreated to gain strength, then struck when the moment came. His supporters say that it was the only way to win, and that his patience saved Texas.

<!-- block 78 role=content -->
His critics see it differently. They say that he did not plan a battle at San Jacinto from the start, and that he nearly lost his army through delay. They argue that the retreat cost homes and lives, and that he was lucky that Santa Anna made mistakes. A report written by the victor will always try to make the victory look inevitable.

<!-- block 79 role=content -->
A careful reader will keep both views in mind. Houston showed real strengths: he kept his army together, he refused to be rushed, and he fought at the right time. He also took risks, and he benefited from enemy errors. Good history shows how a hard choice looked at the time, not just how it looked afterward.
