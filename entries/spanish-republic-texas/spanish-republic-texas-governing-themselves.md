<!-- section: Keep the Mexican form, put American practice inside it. -->

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
Austin held an extraordinary concentration of power in his colony: he was empresario, judge, and militia commander all at once. Mexican law required a system of local *alcaldes*, elected magistrates, but provided no trial by jury, something Austin's colonists expected as part of their Anglo-American legal tradition. What they built instead is the pattern for the whole quarter: keep the required Mexican form, and put American practice inside it.

<!-- section: PART 1 ONE MAN, THREE JOBS -->

<!-- block 13 role=content -->
Austin combined economic, judicial, and military authority in a single person: empresario over land grants, judge over disputes, and commander of the colony's militia. Concentrating this much power in one office was unusual even by the standards of the time, but it reflected the practical reality of a colony hundreds of miles from any Mexican court or garrison that could otherwise enforce order.

<!-- section: WATCH OUT -->

<!-- block 15 role=content -->
Do not read this concentration of power as Austin seizing control. Mexican law itself created the alcalde system his colony operated under; Austin's outsized role came from distance and necessity, not from stepping outside the legal forms already in place.

<!-- section: PART 2 THE WORKAROUND -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
In January 1824, Austin issued civil and criminal regulations that required disputes to go before an elected **alcalde** (ahl-KAHL-deh), exactly as Mexican law required. But Mexican law provided for no trial by jury, something colonists expected as part of their Anglo-American legal tradition. The workaround: colonists could still request an informal jury of arbitrators, whose verdict the alcalde's tribunal would then formally ratify. The Mexican form stayed intact on paper; the American practice lived inside it.

<!-- block 18 role=content -->
Jury trial did not disappear as an issue once this workaround existed. It becomes a standing grievance, one that reappears in the petitions of 1832 and 1833, and again in the Declaration of 1836. Watch for it.

<!-- section: THE PATTERN TO WATCH -->

<!-- block 20 role=content voice imperative -->
Keep the Mexican form, put American practice inside it. This pattern is exactly how this colony survives inside a legal system that was never built with it in mind, and exactly what starts to strain once Mexico City tightens its grip.
