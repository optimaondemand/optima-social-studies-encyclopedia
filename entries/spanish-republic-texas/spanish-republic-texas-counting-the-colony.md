<!-- section: Three centuries of claims. Three towns. -->

<!-- block 4 role=content tool_mention voice -->
San Antonio de Béxar, La Bahía, Nacogdoches. That is the complete list of towns Spanish Texas ever really produced. Today's lesson does the arithmetic on what three centuries of Spanish claims to Texas actually amounted to, and asks you to judge whether the word "colony" even fits.

<!-- section: PART 1 THE ARITHMETIC -->

<!-- block 13 role=content tool_mention -->
San Antonio de Béxar (1718), La Bahía (1721), Nacogdoches (1779). That is the complete list. By the 1810s, after three centuries of claiming this territory, Spain had fewer than 3,000 non-Indian residents in all of Texas. Try the interactive below and watch that arithmetic build in real time.

<!-- section: PART 2 COLONY, OR GARRISON? -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
A student arguing "Spanish Texas left no lasting mark because so few colonists ever lived there" would be missing something real. The vaquero tradition, ranching, the Spanish land system, water law, and much of the region's place-naming all trace back to this era, even though the population never grew large.

<!-- section: HELPFUL TO KNOW -->

<!-- block 19 role=content -->
"Colony" implies a broad, settled, civilian population engaged in agriculture and trade. "Garrison" implies a few defended military-religious outposts holding a claim, without the population to match it. The evidence is what settles which word actually fits Spanish Texas.
