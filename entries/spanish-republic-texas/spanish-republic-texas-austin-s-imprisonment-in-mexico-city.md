<!-- section: What happens to a patient peacemaker who loses his patience once? -->

<!-- block 26 role=content tool_mention -->
In 1833, Stephen F. Austin carried the colonists' **petitions** to Mexico City. He got part of what he asked for. Then he wrote one hasty letter, and it cost him more than a year of his life. This lesson follows Austin's second trip to the capital and what it did to the most patient man in Texas.

<!-- section: Carrying the petition -->

<!-- block 39 role=content -->
Austin left Texas in the spring of 1833 with the petitions from the convention in his bags. He was the right man for the job. He spoke Spanish well, he knew the officials, and he had spent ten years proving that the colonists could be trusted. When he reached Mexico City in July, he began the same patient work that had succeeded before.

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
He lived modestly in the city and tried to meet as many officials as he could. He also wrote letters home to keep the colonists informed. Many **settlers** did not understand how slowly the Mexican government worked. Austin's letters tried to explain that patience was the price of success.

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
Mexico was in a stormy mood. General **Santa Anna** had been elected president, but he did not like the daily business of governing. He often went home to his estate and left the work to the vice president, **Gómez Farías**, who was busy with **reforms**. Austin had to chase the man in power through a government that kept changing hands.

<!-- block 45 role=content -->
Austin presented the colonists' requests one by one. He asked for the **repeal** of the Law of April 6, 1830. He asked for relief from the **tariffs**. And he asked that Texas be made a state of its own, apart from **Coahuila**. Santa Anna listened, and Austin left some meetings feeling hopeful.

<!-- section: A partial win -->

<!-- block 49 role=content -->
Part of the effort worked. The government agreed to repeal the part of the Law of April 6 that had shut the door to settlers from the United States. Families could once again come to Texas. For the colonists, this was a real victory, and it showed that Austin's patience could still move Mexican officials.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
But the main request failed. Mexico City would not **grant** separate **statehood**. Santa Anna and his advisers said that Texas was still too young and too thinly settled. They urged Austin to wait. They also hinted that things might change once the country grew calmer.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
Think about Austin's position. He had been in the capital for months, and his **colony** was waiting for news. He had won one important point and lost the biggest one. Many people would have come home, reported on what had been won, and tried again later. Austin was tired, worried, and far from home. He decided to push once more.

<!-- section: The letter of October 2 -->

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
On October 2, 1833, Austin wrote a letter to the town council of San Antonio. He told them that Texas had waited long enough. He urged the council to organize a state government on its own, without waiting for permission from Mexico City. He wrote in anger and in haste, worried that more delay would leave Texas with no protection at all.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
It was a very different letter from the careful papers that Austin usually wrote. For ten years he had warned his neighbors against rash steps. Now, in one evening, he had suggested one himself. Austin later called the letter a mistake, and historians have called it his most unwise act.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
The letter did not stay private. The San Antonio council sent it on to the state government, and from there it reached Mexico City. To Mexican officials, a request to organize a state government without permission looked like a plan to break the law. The one man they thought they could trust had put his name to something that frightened them.

<!-- section: Arrest and prison -->

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
Austin started for home in December, thinking that his work was done. In January 1834, at **Saltillo**, he was arrested. The soldiers sent him back to Mexico City under guard. He had not been charged with anything yet, but his freedom was gone.

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
In the capital, Austin was held in a cold stone cell in an old prison. For a time, he was not allowed to write to anyone, and he did not know how long he would be kept. He had gone to Mexico City to carry a request for his neighbors. Now he was a prisoner of the government he had served for a decade.

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
Slowly, conditions improved. He was moved to better rooms, and he was allowed books and visitors. By December 1834, he was released on **bond**. A bond is a promise, backed by money, that a person will return for his trial. But he could not leave the city, because the government had not yet decided what to do with him.

<!-- section: Waiting for amnesty -->

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
For months more, Austin waited in Mexico City for the government to settle his case. In July 1835, a general pardon, called an amnesty, was announced, and he was free to go. He sailed home by way of New Orleans and reached Texas in September 1835. He had been away for more than two years.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
The long wait took its toll. Austin was in poor health after the cold prison and the years of strain. He was also worried about his colony. The settlers who had once looked to him for every decision had been forced to make decisions alone. He was eager to see how much of his work had survived.

<!-- block 67 role=content tool_mention -->
The Texas he found was not the one he had left. Soldiers had been sent to the coast. Neighbors were arguing about whether to submit or to resist. The old friends who had trusted him to bring back peace now wanted to know what he had learned. His answer would shape what Texas did next.

<!-- section: A changed man -->

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
Austin had gone south as a loyal Mexican citizen. He came home convinced that the government in Mexico City could not be trusted to keep its word. He had been jailed without trial for acts that, in his own mind, were attempts to help. He believed that the time for patient requests was over.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
This was a huge change. Austin had spent ten years defending Mexico and urging calm. Now he told his neighbors that Texas must prepare to defend itself. When a man who has always chosen peace says that it is time to act, many listeners pay attention.

<!-- block 71 role=content voice imperative -->
Look at what Austin's story shows. A conciliator is a person who works to bring people together and avoid a fight. Austin was the best conciliator Texas ever had. When even he decided that conciliation had failed, there was little hope left that the quarrel could be settled by talk.

<!-- block 72 role=content -->
His change did not make war certain. Choices still had to be made, by many people on both sides. But after September 1835, the colonists no longer had a leader whose main aim was to avoid a fight. The country was moving toward a crisis, and its calmest voice had gone quiet.
