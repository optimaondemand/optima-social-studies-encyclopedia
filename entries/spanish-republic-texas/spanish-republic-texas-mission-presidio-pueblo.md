<!-- section: The system was designed to work. Where did it actually work? -->

<!-- block 4 role=content voice -->
The mission system had a clear design: congregate a Native population, convert them, tax their labor, and eventually hand the mission over as a regular town. That design worked, imperfectly, in some places and not at all in others. Today you will see exactly where and why.

<!-- section: PART 1 THE DESIGN, AND WHO BUILT IT -->

<!-- block 13 role=content tool_mention -->
The mission system's design was simple on paper: congregate a Native population at the mission, convert them to Christianity, tax their labor to support the mission and the empire, and eventually secularize the mission into an ordinary town under Spanish civil law. Two missionaries deserve to be named directly here: Fray Damián Massanet, who founded the first East Texas missions in 1690, and Fray Antonio Margil de Jesús, who founded San Antonio de Valero and other missions in the years that followed. Their work is the design being tested in this lesson.

<!-- section: PART 2 WHERE IT HALF-WORKED -->

<!-- block 15 role=content -->
Mission life at San Antonio meant daily labor, much of it maintaining the **acequias**, the irrigation ditches that carried water to mission farmland, alongside constant exposure to European diseases the missions never really controlled. The system worked best with Coahuiltecan bands, whose way of life was already disrupted enough by other pressures that mission life offered some real advantages. It worked only partially with the Caddo, whose settled, agricultural, confederated society needed the mission system far less than a mobile band did.

<!-- section: The one wave of civilian colonists -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
In 1731, Spain shipped a group of civilian colonists from the Canary Islands to San Antonio, the only time in three centuries it sent settlers this way. Nearly everything else about Spanish Texas ran on missionaries and soldiers, not ordinary civilian migration.
