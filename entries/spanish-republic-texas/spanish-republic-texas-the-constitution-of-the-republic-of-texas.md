<!-- section: What do you build first when you have just won a country? -->

<!-- block 32 role=content tool_mention -->
After **San Jacinto**, Texas was free, but it had no money, no recognized borders, and no settled government. The people who had fought the war now had to write the rules for a nation. This lesson looks at the **constitution** they wrote, the **treaties** they signed with a captured president, and the first steps of the **Republic**.

<!-- section: A convention in a cold hall -->

<!-- block 45 role=content -->
The constitution of the Republic was written in March 1836, in the same rough hall where **independence** was declared. The delegates worked quickly because **Santa Anna** 's army was on the march. In two weeks, they finished a document that would shape the Republic for its whole life. They approved it on March 17, while their army was **retreating**.

<!-- block 50 role=content -->
An interim government took over until a regular one could be elected. David Burnet became interim president, and Lorenzo de **Zavala**, a Mexican statesman who had joined the Texas cause, became vice president. Their jobs were temporary. They held the country together during the weeks of the war and the first months of peace.

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
The delegates agreed on one thing quickly. Texas needed a government strong enough to wage a war and weak enough that no one man could rule it. Many of them had watched Santa Anna turn a republic into a one-man government. They did not want to repeat that story, and the constitution reflects their fear.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Think about how unusual the moment was. Most of the delegates had arrived in Texas within the last few years. Some had served in state legislatures or in **Congress** in the United States. Now they had to write a charter for a new country while a war went on outside the door.

<!-- section: What they borrowed -->

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
The delegates borrowed heavily from the United States. The constitution divided power among three branches: a president, a congress with two houses, and courts. It included a bill of rights that guarded free speech, trial by **jury**, and the right to bear arms. Anyone who knew the American system would feel at home.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
They also changed some things. The first president served two years and later presidents served three, and no president could be elected again right away. The aim was to prevent one man from holding power for too long. Many delegates remembered Mexico's strongmen, and they wanted a government that could not be taken over by a single leader.

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
The constitution also gave the president real powers. He commanded the army and navy, he made treaties with the approval of the **Senate**, and he named many officials. The delegates believed that a young nation facing danger needed one clear leader, as long as the office had limits.

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
Some features came from Mexico, and they lasted. Texas kept the Spanish and Mexican rules for land **grants**. It kept the idea of community property, which says that what a husband and wife earn during marriage belongs to both of them. This was a legal tradition from Spain, and it protected the rights of wives in ways that many American states did not yet do.

<!-- section: Slavery written in -->

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
The constitution also protected slavery. It said that Congress could not pass any law to stop slaveholders from bringing **enslaved** people into Texas, that Congress had no power to free enslaved people, and that no owner could free them without the consent of Congress, unless the person was sent out of the country. It said that free Black people could not live in Texas without the consent of Congress.

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
This matters for understanding the people of the Republic. They did not all agree about slavery, and many **Texians** owned no enslaved people at all. But the law of the land protected the practice, and the economy of the cotton farms along the rivers rested on it.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
This was a clear break from Mexican law, which had moved toward ending slavery. For many of the delegates, protecting slavery was part of what they wanted from independence. The Republic's founders did not hide the matter. They wrote it into the document. That fact would shape the later history of the state in many ways.

<!-- section: The Treaties of Velasco -->

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
Meanwhile, the captured Santa Anna was held as a prisoner. In May 1836, at Velasco, he signed two treaties with the Texian government. In the public one, he promised to end the war, to order the Mexican army to withdraw south of the **Rio Grande**, and to free Texian prisoners.

<!-- block 69 role=content -->
Think about what Santa Anna wanted. He wanted to save his life and return to power. Texians wanted him alive as a bargaining tool, though they did not trust him, and some of the soldiers wanted him hanged. Houston kept him safe. Santa Anna was released in the end, after a trip to Washington.

<!-- block 70 role=content -->
In a secret treaty, he promised to work to persuade Mexico to recognize the independence of Texas. The details of the border were left unclear. The agreement gave Texians something that they wanted. But it was a promise from a man who was a prisoner and who could easily say that he had signed under pressure.

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
That is just what the Mexican government said. It declared that a **captive** president had no power to give away Mexican land, and it refused to honor either treaty. Santa Anna was soon sent home. Mexico never accepted the loss of Texas, and for years it talked about winning it back.

<!-- section: Houston as president -->

<!-- block 74 role=content -->
In the fall of 1836, voters elected Sam Houston as the first regular president. They also voted on whether to seek annexation to the United States, and they voted yes by a huge margin. Texians saw union with the United States as the safest future. They did not expect Washington to delay.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
Houston ran a very thin government. He kept the army small, because he knew that the country could not pay for a large one. He tried to keep peace with Mexico by avoiding **raids** across the border. At home he worked with Native peoples when he could, and he tried to avoid costly fights.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
Houston also had to deal with the army. Soldiers who had won at San Jacinto were restless and hoped to march into Mexico. Houston sent most of them home on leave, because the country could not feed or pay them. It was an unpopular choice, but it kept the **treasury** from collapsing even faster.

<!-- block 77 role=content -->
In March 1837, the United States recognized Texas as a nation. It was a great relief. But Washington refused to take the next step. Many Americans in the North feared that adding Texas would add a huge slave state. President Van Buren did not want a war with Mexico. In 1838, Texas withdrew its offer.
