<!-- section: Debts from the start -->

<!-- block 52 role=content -->
The Republic began its life in debt. The war had cost money for soldiers, for supplies, and for ships. Texas had borrowed from banks and individuals in the United States, and it had promised to pay them back. But it had almost no income. There was no gold or silver mine, and few people to tax.

<!-- block 53 role=content -->
The debt grew year by year. By the early 1840s, the Republic owed millions of dollars, far more than it could ever raise from its small population. Interest piled up on top of the debt. A country that cannot pay its bills finds that credit dries up, and then even small needs become hard to meet.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Think about the size of the country. Fewer than forty thousand people lived in Texas in the first years of the Republic. They were farmers and merchants who had barely started to build their homes. They could not pay for an army, a navy, a government, and the roads and offices that a country needs.

<!-- block 55 role=content -->
The government tried several ways to raise money. It charged **tariffs** on goods coming in through the ports, but smugglers avoided them. It levied direct taxes, but they were hard to collect over so much distance. It sold **bonds**, but few buyers wanted them. Each fix brought in a little and left a large gap.

<!-- section: The collapse of the redbacks -->

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
When money ran short, the Republic printed more. Paper notes were issued in the late 1830s, and the later ones were known as **redbacks**, because of the color of the ink on the back. At first they were accepted at close to their face value. But the government kept printing, and people lost trust.

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
As more redbacks flooded the market, each one bought less. A note that was worth a dollar in 1839 might buy only a few cents' worth by 1842. Merchants refused to take it, or took it only at a deep discount. Soldiers and officials were paid in money that lost its value before they could spend it.

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
Think about a family holding these notes. A farmer sold a wagon of corn and received paper. When he went to buy tools, the merchant said that the paper was worth only half of what it claimed. By the time he could buy anything else, it was worth even less. Each day of waiting cost him more.

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
This is a common fate of a government that spends more than it earns. It is tempting to print money to **cover** the gap. But when money is printed faster than goods are made, the money buys less. Texas learned that lesson the hard way, and the damage fell on ordinary families who held the notes.

<!-- section: What Texas could offer -->

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
If Texas had one real treasure, it was land. The country claimed an enormous area, and most of it was empty of settlers. The government could not pay its bills in cash, but it could pay them in acres. Land became a kind of **currency**, and the Republic used it for almost everything.

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
Soldiers who fought in the war received land, as a **bounty** for their service. Families who were already in Texas at **independence** received large tracts. Later arrivals received a smaller but still generous headright. A family could get over a thousand acres, and a single man could get several hundred.

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
That offer pulled in settlers by the thousands. Families from the southern United States crossed the Sabine with wagons and hopes. Some came to escape debts at home. Others wanted to farm on a scale that would have been impossible in the older states. Land was the Republic's best advertisement, and it worked.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
Other costs came with the offer. The government had to survey land, record titles, and settle disputes among claimants. Many claims overlapped, and many titles were poorly drawn. Lawsuits over land would fill Texas courts for generations. A promise of land is easy to make and hard to keep clean.

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
But land had a weakness as money. It could not be spent quickly, it was hard to sell, and its value depended on how safe the country seemed. A buyer does not pay much for ground that **Comanche** **raiders** or Mexican armies might take. Land made Texas rich on paper, but it did not fill the **treasury**.

<!-- section: A new capital on the edge -->

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
The first capital had been the town of Houston, on Buffalo **Bayou**, a muddy new town that grew quickly. Officials complained of mud, mosquitoes, and high prices. When **Congress** voted to move, it was choosing the future of the country as much as the site of a town.

<!-- block 72 role=content -->
In 1839, the Republic chose a new capital. Congress picked a spot on the **Colorado** River, in the rolling country where the settled farms ended and the Comanche range began. The new town was named Austin, in honor of the founder of the **colony**. It was a bold choice, and a dangerous one.

<!-- block 75 role=content -->
The leaders hoped that a capital on the **frontier** would encourage settlers to push farther west. But the town was exposed. Government clerks worked with **rifles** close at hand, and travelers to Austin faced the risk of raids. For years, the capital sat at the very edge of the country that it governed.

<!-- section: The Cherokee in East Texas -->

<!-- block 78 role=content -->
One land question could not be solved by a headright. The Cherokee people, led by Chief Bowles, had lived in East Texas since the 1820s. They had come from east of the Mississippi, and they had settled the land with the permission of Mexican officials. They farmed and built homes, and they wanted to keep them.

<!-- block 79 role=content -->
In February 1836, as the war began, Houston had signed a **treaty** with the Cherokee and several other Native groups. It recognized their claim to a tract of land in East Texas, in return for peace during the war. Houston, who had lived among the Cherokee as a young man, hoped to keep them as friends.

<!-- block 80 role=content -->
But the treaty needed the approval of the **Senate**, and the Senate would not give it. Many **Texians** believed that the Cherokee land should be open to settlers, and some argued that the Cherokee had no right to it at all. The treaty was never approved, and the Cherokee claim sat in legal limbo.

<!-- block 81 role=content -->
That was where matters stood in December 1838, when Houston's term ended and a new president took office. The question of who owned East Texas was still open. A new president with a different idea about the Native peoples of Texas would soon decide to settle the question by force.
