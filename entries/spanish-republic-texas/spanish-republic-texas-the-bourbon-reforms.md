<!-- section: A slow reform hollows out an empire before anyone notices. -->

<!-- block 4 role=content -->
Starting in the mid-1700s, Spain's Bourbon monarchy pursued a set of reforms aimed at centralizing authority, taxing more efficiently, professionalizing government, and curbing the Catholic Church's independent power. On this frontier, the effect was a Texas weaker in 1800 than it had been in 1750.

<!-- section: PART 1 WHAT THE REFORMS WERE -->

<!-- block 13 role=content -->
The Bourbon Reforms aimed to centralize government authority, collect taxes more efficiently, professionalize colonial administration, and curb the independent power the Catholic Church had built up over centuries. On paper, this was meant to make the Spanish empire stronger and better managed.

<!-- section: PART 2 WHO GOT SQUEEZED, AND WHAT IT COST THE FRONTIER -->

<!-- block 15 role=content -->
**Criollos**, people of Spanish descent born in the Americas, found themselves increasingly excluded from official positions in their own country as Spanish-born officials were favored under the more centralized system. That exclusion bred real resentment among a class of people with genuine standing to expect a voice in their own governance.

<!-- section: WATCH OUT -->

<!-- block 17 role=content -->
This is exactly what happens when self-government is denied to people capable of exercising it. That resentment does not stay contained to Mexico City; it has real consequences for a frontier province that depends on steady attention and resources from the center.

<!-- block 18 role=content -->
On this frontier specifically, the reforms reinforced Rubí's line: missions were abandoned, and defense contracted further inward. Spanish Texas enters the 1800s weaker than it had stood in 1750, the direct legacy of a reform meant to strengthen the empire that governed it.
