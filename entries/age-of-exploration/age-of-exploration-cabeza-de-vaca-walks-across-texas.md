<!-- section: Eight years on foot, across a land no European had ever crossed. -->

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
After the shipwreck, **Cabeza de Vaca** lived among the peoples of Texas for years. He was a slave, then a trader, then a **healer**. At last he walked west with three other survivors until he reached Spanish soil again. When he finally told his story, it sent Spaniards searching for riches in the north.

<!-- section: A way to survive -->

<!-- block 37 role=content -->
Cabeza de Vaca spent about six years living among the peoples of the Texas coast and the country beyond it. At first he was **enslaved**. Then he found a way to change his place in the world. He escaped from his captors and became a trader. He carried sea shells and other coastal goods inland, and he brought back animal hides, flint for tools, and other things that people wanted.

<!-- block 40 role=content -->
A trader had one great advantage. He was not a threat. A man who carried shells and flint, and who belonged to no group, was welcome in places where an armed stranger would have been driven away. This freedom let him see more of the country than any European had seen before. It also taught him how different peoples depended on each other for things they could not make or find at home.

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
Those years were hard. He wrote that he went hungry, worked at hard tasks like digging roots, and often had little clothing in cold weather. He was far from home with no way to send word.

<!-- block 42 role=content -->
This kind of trade is called **barter**. Because he was a stranger who belonged to no group, he could travel between peoples who might otherwise have kept apart. His travels took him far beyond the coast where the **Karankawa** lived.

<!-- section: The healer -->

<!-- block 46 role=content voice -->
People also asked him to heal them. He wrote that he and his companions prayed over the sick, made the sign of the cross, and used simple treatments. Many people recovered, or so he said. We cannot know what really happened in each case. What we do know is that word spread. Groups began to welcome the strangers as healers, giving them food and guides.

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
As their fame grew, crowds of people began to travel with them from town to town. Villages gave them food and gifts, and each new group handed them on to the next. In this way, a few starving strangers became honored guests.

<!-- block 48 role=content -->
It is worth thinking about how this looked from the other side. The people who welcomed the four men had their own ideas about sickness and healing. They saw strangers who seemed to have power, and they decided that it was wise to treat such people well. Their careful choices were part of what kept the travelers alive.

<!-- section: The long walk -->

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
In 1532, Cabeza de Vaca met three other survivors of the **expedition**. Two were Spanish officers, **Dorantes** and **Castillo**. The third was **Estevanico**, an enslaved man from Morocco in North Africa. The four had been held by different groups. They agreed to escape together, and in 1534 they set out to the west.

<!-- block 56 role=content -->
They walked for about two years across what is now Texas and northern Mexico. Historians still argue about their exact **route**. Along the way, they passed through many different groups, each with its own food, customs, and language. They had no map. They depended on the kindness of strangers and on their growing fame as healers.

<!-- block 57 role=content voice -->
The route is hard to trace because Cabeza de Vaca gave few place names, and the land has changed since then. Some historians place the travelers along the Texas coast and then across the middle of the state. Others place them farther north or south. Nobody can be sure, which reminds us how much of this story rests on a single book.

<!-- block 58 role=content voice -->
On the way, the travelers saw huge herds of animals that Europeans had never described before. Cabeza de Vaca called them "cows with humped backs." We call them **bison**. His book is one of the first European descriptions of them.

<!-- block 59 role=content voice -->
These details mattered because they were new to European readers. Nobody in Spain had ever seen a bison, so Cabeza de Vaca had to describe one using animals his readers knew. He compared it to the cattle of home. Writers who meet a strange thing often do this, and it shows how hard it is to describe something that has no name in your own language.

<!-- block 60 role=content -->
In 1536, they met a party of Spanish soldiers in western Mexico. Cabeza de Vaca had been gone for eight years. The soldiers could hardly believe who he was.

<!-- block 61 role=content -->
The Spanish soldiers they met were hunting for Native people to make slaves. Cabeza de Vaca was angry. He had spent years among these peoples, and he knew what captivity felt like. He argued with the soldiers and tried to protect the people who had helped him.

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
What Cabeza de Vaca had lived through changed his mind about many things. He had been treated as a slave, and he had also been treated as a guest, and he knew both feelings. He later argued that Native peoples should be won by kindness, not by force. Not everyone in the Spanish system agreed with him.

<!-- section: What Spain heard -->

<!-- block 65 role=content -->
When the four reached Mexico City, they told the **viceroy** what they had seen. They described many peoples, large towns in some places, and stories of rich lands to the north. They had not seen cities of gold. But Spain was hungry for another Mexico, and people tend to hear what they hope to hear. The report led the viceroy to send out new explorers, and Estevanico would soon be one of them. The search that followed is a story of its own.

<!-- block 66 role=content -->
Cabeza de Vaca also wrote a book. It was printed in 1542 as **The Account** (*La Relación*). It is one of the earliest written descriptions of Texas and its peoples.

<!-- section: What does he notice? What does he assume? -->

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
His book is a primary source, which means it was written by someone who was there. Primary sources are powerful, but they are not neutral. Cabeza de Vaca wrote for the Spanish king. He described the people he lived with in careful detail. He wrote about what they ate, how they dressed, and where they traveled. In some passages, he treats them with real respect.

<!-- block 72 role=content voice -->
But he also took many things for granted. He believed that the Spanish faith was true and that Spanish ways were the standard. He often measured others by that standard. When you read him, you can ask two questions. What does he notice? And what does he assume without saying so?

<!-- block 73 role=content voice -->
Asking those questions turns a story into evidence. It can help you see the people he met, including the Karankawa, more clearly than he saw them himself.
