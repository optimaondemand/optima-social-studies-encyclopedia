<!-- section: What did Spain actually win when it took Mexico? -->

<!-- block 25 role=content tool_mention -->
When **Cortés** took **Tenochtitlán**, Spain won something much bigger than one city. It took over a working system for collecting wealth from millions of people. This lesson looks at how that system worked, how the Spanish **Crown** tried to govern it, and what made it succeed in central Mexico. It also looks at a problem hidden inside its success.

<!-- section: What Spain really captured -->

<!-- block 38 role=content -->
The **Mexica** had a system. Millions of farmers grew crops, and officials kept records of who owed what to whom. Each year, towns sent **tribute** to the capital in goods and in labor. Cortés and the Spaniards did not have to invent any of this. They took over much of the old system and put themselves at the top. In that sense, the real prize was not the city of Tenochtitlán. It was the people, and the work they already knew how to do.

<!-- block 43 role=content -->
The Spaniards also ruled through Native leaders. Local leaders who agreed to cooperate often kept some of their position and collected the tribute for their new rulers. This saved Spain the trouble of governing thousands of towns directly. Over time, the capital of the Mexica became the capital of New Spain, and Mexico City grew on top of the old city.

<!-- block 44 role=content -->
This was a clever way to rule, and it also shows how small the Spanish group was. There were only a few thousand Spaniards in all of New Spain in the early years, set among millions of Native people. A government that small could not run everything itself. It needed local leaders who knew the people, the languages, and the land.

<!-- section: Labor as a reward -->

<!-- block 47 role=content -->
To make the system work, Spain used the **grant** of Native labor (*encomienda*). The Crown gave a Spaniard the right to collect tribute and labor from a group of Native families in a certain area. That Spaniard was called an **encomendero**. In return, he promised to protect those families and to teach them the Christian faith. He did not receive the land itself. He received the right to the work.

<!-- block 51 role=content -->
In practice, many holders treated the workers harshly. Workers faced long hours in the fields and mines, and diseases like **smallpox** killed huge numbers of them. The Crown worried about this, partly for moral reasons and partly because it did not want its colonists to grow too powerful. In 1512, even before the conquest of Mexico, Spain issued the Laws of **Burgos**. They set rules for how workers should be housed, fed, and taught. But the laws did not end the system. They accepted it.

<!-- block 54 role=content -->
Not everyone in the Spanish world agreed with the system. A **friar** named **Las Casas**, who had once held an encomienda himself, spoke out against its cruelty. He argued before the king that Native people had rights and should be treated as free subjects. His writing was widely read, and it helped push the Crown to pass new laws.

<!-- block 57 role=content -->
The debate over how to treat Native people was not a side issue. Spanish priests and officials argued about it, and the king listened. That fact surprises many students. But arguing about a rule is different from changing a system. The laws changed on paper, and the people who gained from the system pushed back hard.

<!-- block 58 role=content -->
In 1542, new laws tried to stop encomiendas from being passed down from father to son. Colonists protested loudly, and the Crown softened the rules. Over the next century, the encomienda faded. Other forms of forced labor took its place, but the pattern stayed the same. Spaniards ruled, and Native workers did the work.

<!-- block 59 role=content -->
Why did colonists fight so hard to keep the system? Because the encomienda was their pay. A soldier who had risked his life in the conquest expected a reward, and a grant of workers was the reward he wanted. Take it away, and many of them would have wondered why they had come.

<!-- section: A viceroy and a church -->

<!-- block 62 role=content -->
Spain was thousands of miles away, so it needed someone to speak for the king. In 1535, the Crown created the **Viceroyalty** of New Spain. A viceroy ruled it from Mexico City. The viceroy collected taxes, appointed officials, and answered to the king.

<!-- block 63 role=content -->
The Church was just as important. Groups of **missionaries** arrived in Mexico in the 1520s. They built churches, set up schools, and worked to teach Native people the Christian faith. Government and Church worked side by side, and both depended on a large population living in settled towns.

<!-- block 64 role=content -->
The system also reached north. In 1546, Spaniards found rich silver at a place called **Zacatecas**. Mines need many workers, so people and supplies moved north to the new mines. Notice the pattern. Spain moved toward people it could put to work and wealth it could carry home.

<!-- block 67 role=content -->
The Crown also wanted its share. It took one fifth of all the silver that came out of the mines. This tax was called the royal fifth. Silver mined in Mexico was shipped across the ocean to Spain, and it paid for Spain's wars and its fleets.

<!-- block 68 role=content -->
Think about what that means for a king. Silver from Mexico paid his soldiers and his sailors, and it let him fight wars in Europe. A place that sent silver home was worth defending and worth the cost of governing. A place that sent nothing was not. This simple idea explains many Spanish choices, including the ones about Texas.

<!-- section: What if there is no system to take over? -->

<!-- block 71 role=content -->
The Spanish model needed three things: a large settled population, a tribute system to take over, and wealth worth collecting. Central Mexico had all three. But what would happen if Spain reached a place that had none of them?

<!-- block 72 role=content -->
The pattern is easy to state. Spain went where there were many people, a tribute system, and wealth. It did not go where those things were missing. That is why central Mexico filled with towns and churches, while other parts of the **empire** stayed almost empty for a very long time.

<!-- block 73 role=content -->
In 1519, the same year Cortés landed in Mexico, a Spanish sailor named **Piñeda** sailed along the Gulf of Mexico coast and drew the first known map of it. His map included the coast of what is now Texas. He found no great cities and no farmers paying tribute. There was nothing for an encomendero to collect and no ruler to replace.

<!-- block 76 role=content -->
The same problem shows up at other far edges of the Spanish empire, such as the dry lands of northern Mexico. A model built to take over an existing system struggles where no such system exists. Keep that question in mind as the story moves toward Texas.
