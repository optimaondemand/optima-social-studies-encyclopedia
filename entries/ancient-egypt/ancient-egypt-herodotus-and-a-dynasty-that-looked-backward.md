Herodotusheh-ROD-uh-tusa Greek writer of the fifth century BC, often called the Father of History for his careful, questioning approach to the past., born in the Greek city of Halicarnassus in the fifth century BC, is often called the "Father of History." That title is not because he was the first person ever to write about the past. It is because he was the first to approach it the way modern historians try to. He traveled to see things for himself. He asked more than one source. He told his readers plainly when he was unsure or doubtful, rather than just repeating whatever he had been told as settled fact. This careful, questioning approach to the past is the start of what historians today call historiographyhis-tor-ee-OG-ruh-feethe study of how history gets written, and of which sources deserve trust.. Historiography is the study of how history gets written and which sources deserve trust. In other words, it asks two questions at the same time: what happened, and how do we actually know that? His book, the *Histories*, gives an entire section, Book II, to Egypt.

<!-- block 26 role=content voice -->
Herodotus deserves careful reading, not blind trust. He really did travel, and he really did question Egyptian priests and other local sources. Much of what he reports about Egyptian customs, worship, and the Nile's flooding holds up well against what archaeology and Egypt's own sources confirm. In other words, when we can check his work against other evidence, he usually comes out looking honest and careful. But he was also a foreigner working through translators. He wrote centuries after some of the events he describes. He was willing to pass along stories he found amazing, even when he openly doubted them himself. Reading him well means learning to tell these two kinds of things apart.

<!-- section: TWO KINDS OF CLAIM -->

<!-- block 28 role=content voice imperative -->
Consider two examples from his own text. Herodotus lists three different Greek ideas for why the Nile floods every year on a steady summer schedule. One idea blamed strong seasonal winds pushing the river back on itself. A second blamed melting snow far to the south. A third pointed to a great ocean Greeks believed circled the whole world. Herodotus argues against the first two ideas. He leans toward a version closer to the third. He weighs each one and offers his own reasoned judgment, a truly historical way of handling a real puzzle. Modern science instead credits heavy summer rains falling on the Ethiopian highlands, far south of Egypt, feeding the river until it rises and spreads across its banks each year. But he also repeats a story about a phoenix bird that is said to carry its dead father's body, wrapped in sweet-smelling resin, from Arabia to the temple of the sun once every five hundred years. This is a story he tells his readers he does not fully believe. He adds only that this is what the Egyptians themselves say. That one small word, "say," carried honestly through a whole text, is itself a piece of historical method worth noticing. Herodotus is not lying to us. He is showing his work. In other words, he lets his readers see the seam between what he checked himself and what he only heard from someone else. That seam is exactly what good historical reading looks for.

Egypt's own story continues past the Kushite pharaohs. After Assyria's sack of Thebes in 663 BC weakened Kushite rule, a new Egyptian dynasty rose from the Delta city of **Sais**, a period historians call the Saite DynastySAY-itethe family of Egyptian kings who ruled from the Delta city of Sais, about 664 to 525 BC, known for reviving old Egyptian art styles. (664 to 525 BC). Saite kings, unlike most earlier pharaohs, welcomed Greek traders and soldiers into Egypt. They even let Greek merchants build their own trading town, called Naukratis, in the Nile Delta. This new openness to outsiders is likely a real reason Herodotus, a Greek himself, could travel through Egypt so freely two centuries later. His own visit to Egypt likely came during or just after this era, which helps explain how much he was able to see and record for himself.

<!-- block 34 role=content -->
The Saite pharaohs are worth noticing for a reason that runs through ancient history: not everything in history moves forward. Saite artists and builders studied and copied the styles of the Old Kingdom on purpose. That was the pyramid age of the Old Kingdom. They copied ancient tomb writings on purpose. They brought back nearly thousand year old art styles by choice, down to fine details of pose and dress found on tomb walls far older than their own time. This was not laziness or a lack of new ideas. It was a planned choice: a civilization looking to its own deep past for the right to rule and a sense of steadiness during an uncertain present. It is exactly the kind of pattern worth remembering. History is not one long climb toward something better. Sometimes a civilization's most confident move is to look backward rather than forward. In other words, the Saite kings were not stuck in old habits by accident. They chose to look backward on purpose, because Egypt's own deep past gave them a kind of strength that a brand new style never could.

<!-- block 35 role=content -->
Egypt's own freedom, however, was not built to last. Beyond its borders, first Assyria, and later a much larger power still, was changing the whole Near East. Egypt would soon be pulled into that story directly, when Persia conquered it. For now, the story turns north and east, to the empire whose rise Egypt's own weakness helped make possible. In other words, as Egypt grows weaker at home, somewhere else, a new power is quietly growing stronger.
