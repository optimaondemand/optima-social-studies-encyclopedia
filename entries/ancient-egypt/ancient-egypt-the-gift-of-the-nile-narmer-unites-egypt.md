<!-- section: Why did Egypt unify almost overnight, while Mesopotamia took centuries? -->

<!-- block 13 role=content -->
Mesopotamia offers a useful contrast. It was a land built from two rivers that could flood without warning. It had dozens of cities fighting for power, with centuries of shifting rule between them. Egypt tells a nearly opposite story from its very start. One river, the Nile RiverNYLEthe long river running through Egypt from south to north, flooding its banks every year and making farming possible in an otherwise dry land., floods on such a steady schedule that Egyptian farmers could set their whole year by it. One long strip of farmland, boxed in by desert on both sides, made outside attack harder. It also made it easier to picture the land as one single place. Sumer took centuries to slowly crawl toward kingship. Egypt's story is not like that. It opens with one sharp act of unity that historians can almost point to like the starting shot of a race.

<!-- section: A RIVER THAT KEEPS ITS PROMISES -->

<!-- block 24 role=content -->
Every year, snowmelt and rains far to the south sent the Nile rising over its banks. It rose each summer, then fell back each fall. This flood, called the inundationin-un-DAY-shunthe yearly flood of the Nile River, which covered nearby farmland with rich, dark soil., left behind a fresh layer of dark, rich soil across the farmland. Egyptians called their own land Kemet, "the black land," for that soil. They called the desert beyond it deshret, the "red land." This steady pattern mattered a great deal. A Mesopotamian farmer faced a river that might flood too hard, or fail to rise at all, in a given year. A Nile farmer, for most of Egyptian history, could count on the flood arriving close to the same weeks each year. It usually began in June and peaked by September, then pulled back in time for planting.

<!-- block 25 role=content -->
Egyptian officials even built stone gauges called nilometers along the riverbank to measure the flood's height each year and guess how good the harvest would be. One famous nilometer still stands today on the island of Elephantine, near modern Aswan, marked with lines that once tracked flood heights year after year. Farming itself did not begin in Egypt on its own, though. It reached the Nile Valley from the Fertile Crescent, the region where farming first took hold. It spread south and west by around 5200 BC. Egypt's true skill was not inventing farming. It was fitting farming, with real success, to a kind and steady river.

<!-- section: TWO LANDS BECOME ONE -->

<!-- block 27 role=content -->
For centuries before it became one kingdom, Egypt was really two lands. Upper Egyptthe southern half of Egypt, a narrow strip of farmland along the river valley. lay to the south, a string of towns strung along the narrow river valley. Lower Egyptthe northern half of Egypt, centered on the wide, marshy Nile Delta. lay to the north, centered on the wide, marshy Nile Deltathe fan-shaped area where the Nile splits into many smaller channels before reaching the Mediterranean Sea., where the river fans out toward the Mediterranean Sea. Around 3100 BC, a ruler most historians call Narmer🔊NAR-merthe ruler most historians credit with joining Egypt's two lands into one kingdom, around 3100 BC. brought these two lands under one crown.

<!-- block 28 role=content tool_mention -->
This event is shown on the **Narmer Palette**, a carved stone slab found at Hierakonpolishy-air-uh-KON-poh-lisa city in Upper Egypt where the Narmer Palette was found. in Upper Egypt. It shows a king wearing the White Crown of Upper Egypt on one side and the Red Crown of Lower Egypt on the other. On both sides, he is shown striking down a bound enemy in a formal pose of conquest. The Palette also carries one of the very first known cases of a king's name written down in Egypt, framed inside a small box above his figure. Some historians think an earlier ruler, known only as Scorpion from a second carved macehead, may have begun the work of joining Egypt that Narmer later finished, or simply claimed credit for. Whether the Palette records one clear war, a longer process of joining squeezed into one single picture, or something in between is a real debate among historians today.

<!-- section: MEMPHIS AND A KING FROM THE START -->

<!-- block 35 role=content -->
After Egypt was joined, a new capital, **Memphis**, was built near the border of Upper and Lower Egypt. This choice was meant to stand for the joining of the two lands. From nearly this same starting point, Egyptian kingship took a shape unlike anything Mesopotamia had built. Sumerian and Akkadian kings built their power slowly. They often still had to answer to priests or councils of elders. Kingship itself only slowly pulled apart from temple rule in Mesopotamia.

<!-- block 36 role=content -->
Egypt's king, called pharaohFAIR-ohthe title used for the king of Egypt in later periods of its history. in later times, was treated from a very early point as a living god. He was seen as an image of the falcon god Horus on earth, in charge of keeping cosmic order and justice🔊MAH-aht (ma'at)the truth and balance that ancient Egyptians believed kept the whole world working the right way, a duty every pharaoh had to protect.: the cosmic order, truth, and balance that kept the whole world running the right way. The word pharaoh itself first meant "great house," a name for the royal palace, before it later came to mean the king himself. Dynastic Egypt, once begun, would go on to last more than three thousand years, one of the longest runs of any civilization in world history. This is a truly different idea of kingship. It is not a man chosen by the gods to rule, as Hammurabi would later claim. It is a man who was, in some real sense, seen as divine himself. Egyptian writing, called hieroglyphicshy-ruh-GLIF-iksEgypt's early writing system, made of small pictures that stood for sounds, words, or ideas., appears on royal monuments like the Narmer Palette from this same early period. It records the names and titles of kings almost from the moment Egypt was joined.

<!-- section: PEOPLES THE EGYPTIANS -->

<!-- block 39 role=content -->
Early Egyptians wore simple linen clothing suited to a hot, dry climate: kilts for men, close-fitting dresses for women, and wide bead collars for the wealthy. Eye makeup made from ground minerals was common for both men and women, worn to guard against sun glare and eye disease as much as for looks.

<!-- section: A QUESTION WORTH HOLDING ONTO -->

<!-- block 41 role=content -->
This difference is worth sitting with rather than rushing past. Why might one river valley civilization arrive at total, sudden divine kingshipdih-VYNE KING-shipthe belief that a ruler is not just chosen by the gods, but is in some real way a god. almost from its first united moment? Why did another spend centuries working out the pull between kings and priests, cities and empires? Geography answers part of the question. Egypt's desert walls on both sides gave a joining king fewer rivals to answer to than a Mesopotamian ruler ringed by rival cities and open plains. But geography alone does not settle the matter.
